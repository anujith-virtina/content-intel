# Audience — eldercarematters

There are two audiences and they want opposite things. Almost all blog content is written for the first.

## Primary persona: the adult child

A working adult, typically 45 to 65, making care decisions for a parent. Often a daughter, often the sibling who lives closest, often doing this alongside a job and children of their own.

### Their situation

- The decision is usually triggered by an event: a fall, a hospital discharge, a diagnosis, a spouse dying
- They are on a deadline they did not choose
- They are spending their parent's money, or their own, and are anxious about both
- Siblings frequently disagree, sometimes from another state
- The parent may not want any of this

### What they already know

- Their parent's health situation, in detail
- That care is expensive
- That they are behind on figuring this out

### What they do not know or get wrong

- The difference between assisted living, memory care, skilled nursing, and home care
- That Medicare does not pay for long-term custodial care. This is the single most common and most expensive misunderstanding.
- That Medicaid eligibility has look-back rules that punish last-minute asset moves
- What questions to ask on a tour
- That care needs change, so the first choice is rarely the last

### Reading context

Phone, evenings and weekends, often in a hospital corridor or after the parent has gone to bed. Ten minutes at most. They are scanning for the one answer they came for, so the answer must be near the top of its section.

### What they hate

- Being sold to while they are frightened
- Vague reassurance with no specifics
- Cost pages that will not name a range
- Content that assumes money is not a constraint
- Being made to feel guilty

### Reading level

General adult. Define every acronym on first use: ADL, SNF, LTC, POA, PACE, CCRC. Do not assume familiarity with Medicare parts, Medicaid waivers, or VA benefits.

## Secondary persona: the provider

Elder law attorneys, home care agencies, senior living communities, and care managers who pay for directory listings. They read the blog to judge whether the site is credible enough to be worth a listing.

They are not the reader the content is written for, but they should never see anything that embarrasses them: no wrong law, no wrong Medicare rules, no invented statistics.

## Geographic scope

National, US only. The site positions itself as America's national directory with no state focus. Do not write state-specific content unless asked, and when a rule genuinely varies by state, say so rather than picking one state's version.

## What they should be able to do after reading

Every post should leave the reader able to take one concrete next action: a question to ask, a document to find, a number to calculate, or a type of professional to call.
