# Voice — eldercarematters

Derived from the live site on 2026-09-01, primarily from post 283258 (`/assisted-living-vs-home-care/`), which is the current house standard. Older posts (280614, 280620) are thinner and should not be copied.

## Tone

Calm, practical, and warm without being sentimental. The reader is usually an adult child under stress, often making this decision for the first time, sometimes in a hurry. Write like a knowledgeable friend who has helped other families through the same decision.

- Informative, never clinical
- Compassionate, never pitying
- Plain language over industry vocabulary
- Never alarmist. The subject is frightening enough without help.

## Person

- Second person for the reader: "you", "your parent", "your family"
- Say "your parent" or "your loved one", not "the elderly person" or "the patient"
- First person plural ("we") only when speaking as ElderCareMatters
- Never write as an individual expert unless a named, real author is supplied

## The single most important rule: no advice you are not qualified to give

This is YMYL content. Health, legal, and financial decisions for vulnerable people are involved.

- **Never give medical advice.** Explain what a care type is and what questions to ask. Do not recommend treatments, medications, or diagnoses.
- **Never give legal or tax advice.** Medicaid eligibility, trusts, and advance directives are explained in general terms only, always with a line pointing the reader to a licensed elder law attorney.
- **Never state a cost as a fact without a dated, cited source.** Care costs vary hugely by state and year.
- **Never invent a statistic.** If a number cannot be traced to a primary source, cut it.
- Prefer authoritative sources: Medicare.gov, Medicaid.gov, the National Institute on Aging, the Alzheimer's Association, the VA, AARP, CMS.

## Sentence and paragraph rules

- Sentences: average 15 to 20 words. Break anything over 25.
- Paragraphs: 2 to 3 sentences. This audience often reads on a phone, late at night.
- One idea per paragraph
- Lead each section with the direct answer, then explain

## Structure habits that work here

Post 283258 does all of these and they should carry forward:

- A short comparison table when two options are being weighed
- Numbered questions the reader can take to a tour or a phone call
- A decision checklist near the end
- A closing section that answers the title question directly rather than hedging

## Punctuation and formatting

- Em dashes: avoid. Use commas, colons, or periods.
- Semicolons: avoid
- Oxford comma: yes
- Exclamation marks: never
- **Headings: Title Case.** This differs from every other client in this repo and it is deliberate. The live site uses Title Case throughout ("What Is Home Care for Seniors?"). Match the site, not the house default.
- Bold for genuine emphasis only
- No emoji

## Banned words and phrases

AI tells:
delve, leverage, navigate (verb), realm, landscape, ecosystem, "in today's fast-paced world", "it's important to note", "in conclusion", "when it comes to"

Hype:
revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, world-class, best-in-class, "transform your", "peace of mind" as filler

Tone failures specific to this subject:
- "the elderly" as a noun. Use "older adults" or "your parent".
- "suffering from dementia". Use "living with dementia".
- "facility" when "community" or "home" is what the site uses
- "placement" as a euphemism for moving a parent
- Anything implying the reader is failing their parent

## What a good opening looks like

Good: "Choosing care for an aging parent is a major decision. Families often compare assisted living with home care because both can provide daily support."

Bad: "In today's rapidly aging society, senior care has never been more important."
