## Key findings

### Finding 1: The title is unregulated. The underlying licenses are not. That distinction is the whole post.

- Source: [De-mystifying geriatric care managers](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2019/jun/geriatric-care-managers/) — The Tax Adviser (AICPA journal), June 1, 2019
- What it says: The article states plainly that "GCMs are not regulated by any governmental entity" and that anyone may use the title without sanction.
- Corroboration: A California-focused care agency page and multiple career-guidance sources say the same, and no source found says otherwise. One career source claims roughly 40% of employers require certification, which implies most do not. That 40% figure is **[unverified]** and should be cut.
- The nuance that keeps this accurate: NIA describes a geriatric care manager as **usually** a licensed nurse or social worker. So the *person* is often state-licensed in nursing or social work, and those licenses are regulated and disciplinable. The *job title* is not. The practical consequence for a family: the title tells you nothing, so you must ask what license and what certification sit underneath it.
- **Confidence: high on substance, high on the professional-journal citation, moderate on "in most states" phrasing.** I could not find a 50-state survey confirming that zero states license care managers. Recommend the post says "in most states" or, safer, "there is no national license for care managers and no governmental body regulates the title." That phrasing is fully supported.
- Why it matters: This is the single most useful thing an adult child can learn before the first phone call, and no top-ranking article leads with it.

### Finding 2: There are four credentials ALCA itself recognizes, and they certify different things

ALCA's Advanced Professional membership level requires at least one of exactly four certifications. Source: ALCA membership-level and certification pages on aginglifecare.org, read via search result extracts (the domain returns HTTP 403 to automated fetching — see "Couldn't verify" below).

**CMC — Care Manager Certified, from the National Academy of Certified Care Managers (NACCM)**
- NACCM was established in 1995. Source: NACCM materials and a PRWeb release announcing its NCCA accreditation.
- Eligibility is education **plus** paid, full-time, supervised care management experience **plus** direct client contact across all four NACCM content domains: assessment, care planning, execution and monitoring, and professional standards. NACCM's FAQ is blunt that "One's job title alone does not constitute eligibility."
- NACCM also states most coordinator, administrator, or director roles do not count as care management, and experience must fall within the last 10 years, with 50 hours of supervision or consultation per qualifying year.
- Renewal: every three years with continuing education. Source: NACCM accreditation coverage.
- Accredited by the NCCA, the accrediting arm of the Institute for Credentialing Excellence. **[unverified whether accreditation is currently active]** — the announcement I found is undated in the copy I could read.
- What it does NOT certify: it is not a license, it does not confer prescribing or clinical authority, and it does not vet the person's business practices.
- Source: [NACCM FAQs](https://www.naccm.net/about/faqs/), [NACCM application process](https://www.naccm.net/become-certified/application-process/)

**CCM — Certified Case Manager, from the Commission for Case Manager Certification (CCMC), which now brands as The Commission**
- Three requirements: (1) an active unrestricted license or certification in a health or human services discipline, **or** a bachelor's or graduate degree in nursing, social work, or a related field with supervised field experience; (2) employment experience; (3) good moral character under its code of conduct.
- Experience options: 12 months of full-time case management supervised by a CCM, **or** 24 months unsupervised, **or** 12 months supervising people who deliver case management.
- Renewal: every five years, 80 continuing education hours, of which 8 must be ethics. Or retake the exam.
- CCMC materials describe it as the largest case management credentialing body, citing more than 50,000 professionals certified over time. Treat that as an organizational claim, not an independent count. **[unverified]** as a current headcount.
- What it does NOT certify: CCM is a general case management credential, widely held by hospital discharge planners and insurance case managers. It is **not** aging-specific. A CCM may have spent a career in workers' compensation and never worked with an older adult at home. This is a genuinely useful caveat for families and no competitor states it.
- Source: [CCM eligibility fast facts](https://ccmcertification.org/blog/what-you-need-know-about-eligibility-you-apply-ccmr-exam-fast-facts?page=3), [CCMC renewal criteria PDF](https://ccmcertification.org/sites/ccmc/files/docs/2022/CCMC-22-Cert-Guide-Renewal-Update-web.pdf)

**C-SWCM and C-ASWCM — Certified Social Work Case Manager and Certified Advanced Social Work Case Manager, from the National Association of Social Workers**
- C-SWCM is the bachelor's-level social work credential; C-ASWCM is the master's-level one.
- ALCA accepts both at its Advanced Professional level.
- **[unverified]** whether NASW still actively issues both. I could not load socialworkers.org and search results only showed third-party guides. **Recommendation: name them, describe the BSW/MSW distinction, and do not state anything about current availability.**
- Source: [NASW C-SWCM brochure](https://www.socialworkers.org/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=dPAvUInDURE%3D&portalid=0) — could not be fetched; listed for the analyzer, not for linking.

**Underlying licenses: RN, LCSW, LPC, gerontology degrees**
- A nursing or clinical social work license is a state license with a disciplinary record attached. That is a stronger consumer protection than any voluntary certification, because a board can revoke it.
- A gerontology degree is an academic qualification, not a license, and carries no disciplinary body.

### Finding 3: ALCA membership level is a filter families can actually use, and most families do not know the levels exist

- Source: ALCA membership-level and certification pages, aginglifecare.org (403 to automated fetch, read via search extracts)
- **Advanced Professional** — holds a bachelor's, master's, or PhD, has several years of post-degree supervised aging life care experience, **and** holds one of the four approved certifications above.
- **Professional** — associate's through PhD, several years of post-degree supervised experience, **no certification required**.
- **Associate** — a degree, or a nursing diploma, or an LPN, PTA, or OTA license, and a paid role in the field. This is the entry level.
- Practical consequence: "member of the Aging Life Care Association" is not one thing. A family that asks only "are you a member?" learns almost nothing. The question is "at what level, and which certification qualified you for it?"
- ALCA's own boilerplate, dated April 29, 2026, says the association was formed in 1985 and has "nearly 2,000 members nationwide." Source: [ALCA press release, PR Newswire, April 29, 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/aging-life-care-association-responds-to-growing-caregiver-crisis-with-expert-support-302758098.html)
- **Note the scale point for the post:** roughly 2,000 members serving a country with 63 million family caregivers means many families will not find an ALCA member nearby. The post must give a fallback rather than implying ALCA membership is mandatory.

### Finding 4: Neither Medicare nor Medicaid pays. Long-term care insurance and employer EAPs sometimes do.

- Source: National Institute on Aging, "Services for Older Adults Living at Home" (nia.nih.gov). **The page returns HTTP 405 to automated fetching.** Content confirmed through two independent search extractions that agreed.
- NIA states geriatric care managers charge by the hour, that Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for the service, that most private health insurance does not cover it, that most people pay out of pocket, and that long-term care insurance may cover part of it.
- AARP adds a detail nobody else does: some **employee assistance programs** cover part of care management fees, because the service helps a working caregiver stay at work. AARP also states Medigap and Medicare Advantage plans do not cover it.
- Source: [Geriatric Care Managers Advocate for Older Adults](https://www.aarp.org/caregiving/basics/geriatric-care-manager/) — AARP, **updated August 5, 2026**
- Why it matters: the EAP angle is a concrete, free action the reader can take tonight — call their own HR department. That satisfies the "one concrete next action" audience rule.
- **YMYL handling:** state the Medicare and Medicaid non-coverage as fact, attributed in body text to the National Institute on Aging in plain text. Do not link medicare.gov or cms.gov.

### Finding 5: The only defensible hourly figure is AARP's, and it conflicts sharply with older sources

- **AARP, updated August 5, 2026:** $150 to $300 per hour depending on region. Initial assessments typically run 4 to 6 hours of billable time. Some care managers also bill mileage and travel time. AARP tells readers to get the fee structure in writing before agreeing.
- **The Tax Adviser, June 2019:** $50 to $200 per hour, varying by the person's condition, needs, and location. Also advises asking what billing increment is used, 6, 10, or 15 minutes.
- **Guardian Life, undated:** $150 to $350 per hour.
- **Recommendation:** anchor the post on **AARP's $150 to $300, named and dated as AARP, updated August 2026**. Mention the 4-to-6-hour assessment as AARP's figure too, so the reader can estimate an entry cost without us publishing an unsourced "assessment costs $500 to $1,500" number. Several aggregator sites publish that $500 to $1,500 assessment range with **no source at all** — do not use it.
- The billing-increment question from The Tax Adviser is a genuinely sharp interview question and is safe to use as guidance, not as a cost claim.

### Finding 6: Conflict of interest is real, it is written into ALCA's own ethics code, and legislatures are now acting on it

This is the sleeper angle and the strongest differentiator.

**ALCA's Code of Ethics and Standards of Practice (June 2025 revision) addresses it directly.** Read via search extracts; the PDF is member-gated and 403s. It requires the professional to give full disclosure of business, professional, or personal relationships when making referrals, to act with transparency and objectivity so as to avoid actual or perceived conflict of interest, and to base referrals solely on the client's best interest. Where a professional has direct or indirect ownership in, or financial involvement with, a separate business that takes or offers referral fees, fee splitting, or kickbacks, that business must be operated independently and separately from the aging life care practice. It also advises avoiding self-payment arrangements and recommends third-party oversight where a care manager must help a client pay the care manager's own company.

**ALCA has a complaint mechanism.** A Professional Conduct Review Committee reviews formal complaints that a member violated the code, and there is a public Professional Conduct Review Form on aginglifecare.org. This is a concrete consumer remedy that exists **only for ALCA members**. A non-member care manager has no such body. That is a powerful, specific reason to prefer a member, and no competitor article mentions it.

**The wider referral-fee problem, with real legislation:**
- **Georgia SB 439** passed the Senate 2026-03-06 and the House 2026-03-19, unanimously in both chambers, effective **July 1, 2026**. It requires referral agencies referring to assisted living communities and personal care homes to disclose how the business works, that they collect a referral fee on a successful move-in, and that the family may have options beyond what the agency presents. It ties payment of the referral fee to proof the disclosure was made, and caps the referral-to-move-in window at 24 months.
- Sources: [Georgia Public Broadcasting, March 25, 2026](https://www.gpb.org/news/2026/03/25/georgia-one-of-the-first-states-law-protecting-people-seeking-senior-living) (403 to automated fetch), [LegiScan GA SB439](https://legiscan.com/GA/bill/SB439/2025), [Georgia General Assembly SB 439](https://www.legis.ga.gov/legislation/72634)
- **Washington** was first, in 2011, requiring referral companies to disclose fees up front and carry at least $1 million in liability insurance. The bill followed a 2010 Seattle Times investigation into placements at communities with documented care violations. Source: [ElderLawAnswers, April 28, 2011](https://www.elderlawanswers.com/elder-care-referral-services-attracting-increased-scrutiny-9119). **Caution: this article names a blocked competitor. Do not link it, do not name the company. Use the facts only.**
- California, Oregon, Colorado, Arizona, and Maryland are also reported to have disclosure requirements. **[unverified]** — I did not confirm each statute individually. Recommend the post says "a growing number of states" and names only **Georgia and Washington**, which I verified.

**The distinction the post must draw carefully:** these laws regulate **referral and placement agencies**, not care managers. A care manager who is paid only by the family has no referral fee. A care manager who also runs, or is paid by, a placement business does. The reader's job is to find out which one is sitting across the table. That is one question, and it is the most valuable question in the post.

### Finding 7: Free and low-cost care coordination already exists, and most readers do not know it

- Area Agencies on Aging are public or nonprofit agencies designated by each state to serve a defined planning and service area. ACL lists their service coordination types as person-centered counseling, options counseling, care coordination, and care management.
- The Eldercare Locator connects families to their local Area Agency on Aging.
- Sources: [ACL, Area Agencies on Aging](https://acl.gov/programs/aging-and-disability-networks/area-agencies-aging), [Eldercare Locator](https://eldercare.acl.gov/), [Eldercare Locator AAA page](https://eldercare.acl.gov/Public/About/Aging_Network/AAA.aspx)
- Family Caregiver Alliance also notes some community organizations provide care management at lower cost. Source: [FCA, Work and Eldercare](https://caregiver.org/work-and-eldercare)
- Why it matters: this supports the "when you do not need one" section honestly, and it gives the reader a free option before a $150-to-$300-an-hour one. It also matches the ECM brand line of staying neutral and not pushing the expensive choice.
- **Scope limit:** AAA services vary by region and many have waiting lists or income-based targeting. Say services vary by area. Do not promise anyone free care management.

### Finding 8: There is a real, step-by-step verification path, and zero competitors publish it

Every claim a care manager makes can be checked. This belongs in the post as a numbered "verify before you sign" list.

| Claim | How the family verifies it | What it returns |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm a registered nurse" | Nursys QuickConfirm, the national nurse license database run by NCSBN, free to the public | License status and publicly available discipline for participating boards |
| "I'm a licensed clinical social worker" | The state social work board's own lookup. There is **no** national social work license database | Status, license type, expiry, and disciplinary actions in most states |
| "I'm a CMC" | NACCM publishes a monthly Active CMC List PDF, and takes verification requests via a form, answered in 2 to 3 business days | Confirmation of active status. NACCM does **not** release CMCs' contact details |
| "I'm a CCM" | The Commission (CCMC) runs a public CCM verification lookup and a CCM directory | Confirmation of board certification |
| "I'm an ALCA member" | ALCA's Find an Expert member search, searchable by location, shows member level | Membership and the level, which is the part that matters |
| "I follow a code of ethics" | ALCA's Professional Conduct Review Form exists for formal complaints against members | A route to complain, which only exists for members |

- Sources: [NCSBN license verification](https://www.ncsbn.org/nursing-regulation/licensure/license-verification.page), [Nursys](https://www.nursys.com/), [ASWB look up a license](https://www.aswb.org/licenses/protecting-the-public/look-up-license/), [NACCM verify a CMC](https://www.naccm.net/contact/verify-cmc/), [CCM verification](https://yourcommission.org/ccm-lookup), [CCM directory](https://yourcommission.org/ccm-directory)
- **Important operational note for the publisher:** aginglifecare.org returns **HTTP 403** to every automated request. It is not dead, it blocks bots. Per MUST-FOLLOW-RULES section 6, do not link a URL that cannot be verified as resolving. **Name the Aging Life Care Association in plain text** and give its phone number, 520-881-8008, which both NIA and Family Caregiver Alliance publish. Same treatment for nia.nih.gov (405) and gpb.org (403).

### Finding 9: The interview questions, consolidated from three approved sources

Drawn from AARP (updated August 2026), ALCA's own "Selecting an Aging Life Care Professional" guidance, and The Tax Adviser (June 2019). Deduplicated and ordered for a real phone call.

**Credentials and standing**
1. What license do you hold, in which state, and what is your license number?
2. Which of the four recognized care management certifications do you hold, and when does it renew?
3. If you are an ALCA member, at what level?

**Fit and experience**
4. How many clients like my parent have you handled in the last year? (ALCA frames this as matching areas of expertise to your needs.)
5. How long have you worked in this region, and which communities and agencies do you actually know first-hand?

**Capacity and coverage** — the questions almost nobody asks
6. How many active clients do you carry right now?
7. Are you a solo practitioner or part of a larger practice? (ALCA's own suggested question.)
8. Who covers your clients when you are on vacation, ill, or unreachable?
9. What is your after-hours and weekend availability, and what counts as an emergency?

**Money**
10. Is there a fee for the initial consultation, and how much? (ALCA's own suggested question.)
11. What is your hourly rate, and do you bill in 6, 10, or 15 minute increments? (The Tax Adviser.)
12. How many hours does an initial assessment usually take? (AARP notes 4 to 6.)
13. Do you bill mileage, travel time, or phone calls? (AARP and The Tax Adviser both flag this.)
14. Is this hourly, or a retainer, or a monthly package? Can I have the fee agreement in writing before we start? (AARP: get it in writing.)

**Independence** — the section that differentiates this post
15. Do you, your spouse, or your company have any ownership in, or financial relationship with, any home care agency, assisted living community, or placement service?
16. Do you ever receive a referral fee, commission, or any payment from a provider you recommend?
17. Will you put that answer in writing in the engagement agreement?
18. If I do not take your recommendation, does anything change about what you charge me?

**Deliverables and communication**
19. What do I actually receive after the assessment, and by when? Is it a written care plan?
20. How often will you report, in what format, and to whom?
21. How will you keep siblings in other states informed, and will they be copied on everything?
22. What triggers you to call me immediately rather than wait for the next report?
23. May I speak to two families you have worked with for more than six months?

### Finding 10: Red flags, and when to walk away

Derived from the same sources rather than invented. Every item traces to something above.

- Will not name a license, a state, or a license number
- Cannot or will not say which certification they hold, or names one no recognized body issues
- Refuses to put fees in writing, or the fee agreement omits mileage, travel, and phone time (AARP)
- Cannot answer who covers when they are away
- Owns or is paid by a community or agency they recommend, and did not volunteer that (ALCA code of ethics)
- Declines to give references, or all references are recent
- Recommends a specific community on the first call, before an assessment
- Promises a specific outcome, a wait-list bypass, or a Medicaid result
- Pressures a decision on a hospital discharge deadline
- Asks to be given control of your parent's finances, or to be paid from accounts they themselves manage. ALCA's code specifically advises avoiding self-payment and recommends third-party oversight where it cannot be avoided.

### Finding 11: When you do not need one, stated honestly

There is no formal authority that publishes "you do not need a care manager." This section must be built from reasoning grounded in the sourced material, not presented as a cited finding.

Defensible framing: paid care management earns its fee mainly where distance, complexity, conflict, or time are the constraint. NIA frames care managers as especially helpful when a caregiver lives far away. AARP frames the value partly around a working caregiver's time. Where none of those apply — you live nearby, the needs are stable and simple, the family agrees, and you have the hours — an Area Agency on Aging options counseling session may be enough, and it is free or low cost.

Also worth saying: a care manager is often bought in hours, not by the year. A one-time assessment plus a written plan is a legitimate, bounded purchase. Families do not have to commit to ongoing management.

## Conflicts and disagreements

**Hourly rate**
- **Position A** (AARP, updated Aug 2026): $150 to $300
- **Position B** (The Tax Adviser, Jun 2019): $50 to $200
- **Position C** (Guardian Life, undated): $150 to $350
- **Resolution:** age explains most of the gap, and AARP is the only approved, dated, current source. Use AARP's range with explicit attribution and date. Note in the post that rates vary by region and that lower rates exist outside metro areas.

**Is the title regulated?**
- **Position A** (The Tax Adviser, AICPA): no governmental entity regulates GCMs; anyone can use the title
- **Position B** (NIA): a geriatric care manager is *usually* a licensed nurse or social worker
- **Resolution:** both are true and they are not in conflict. The title is unregulated; the underlying professional license usually is regulated. The post must state both in the same breath or it will mislead. This nuance is itself a differentiator.

**Does ALCA membership mean the person is certified?**
- **Position A** (most competitor articles): ALCA membership is the mark of quality
- **Position B** (ALCA's own membership structure): only the Advanced Professional level requires a certification
- **Resolution:** Position B is correct and is sourced to ALCA itself. Competitors are wrong by omission. Strong content point.

## YMYL landmines for this post

1. **Medicare and Medicaid.** Safe to state, attributed to the National Institute on Aging in plain text, that neither pays for geriatric care management. Do **not** extend this to any statement about what Medicare covers generally, and never imply Medicare covers custodial or long-term care.
2. **No Medicaid asset limit, income cap, or look-back period.** If a care manager's Medicaid-planning role comes up, route to an elder law attorney and stop. One sentence.
3. **Every dollar figure needs the name and date in the body text.** Only AARP's $150 to $300 and the 4-to-6-hour assessment clear this bar. Cut everything else.
4. **Do not state the $500 to $1,500 assessment figure.** No traceable source.
5. **Do not publish the Virginia Tech satisfaction percentages.** Underlying study not located, and the only citation is ALCA's own release.
6. **Do not say "geriatric care managers are unlicensed."** Say the *title* is not regulated and there is no national license for it, while noting most hold a state nursing or social work license. The wrong phrasing defames practising licensed professionals, who are also ECM's secondary audience and its paying listers.
7. **Do not imply ALCA membership is legally required or that non-members are unqualified.** With roughly 2,000 members nationwide, many competent care managers are not members. Frame membership as a useful signal and a complaint route, not a licence.
8. **Do not accuse any category of provider of taking kickbacks.** Describe the referral-fee business model neutrally and tell the reader to ask. Legislation exists precisely because disclosure was absent, not because fraud is universal.
9. **Do not name any blocked competitor**, including in the referral-fee discussion where it would be tempting.
10. **Do not recommend a named care manager, practice, or firm** (brand.md). The directory is the recommendation.
11. **No medical advice.** The "match expertise to your parent's needs" question must stay about the care manager's experience, never about conditions or treatment.
12. **No tax advice.** The Tax Adviser is cited for care-manager selection guidance only. Do not touch deductibility of care management fees, even though that article discusses it.
13. **Banned words check for this topic:** avoid "navigate", "landscape", "when it comes to", "peace of mind", "the elderly", "facility" where "community" fits, "placement" as a euphemism. Note that "placement agency" is unavoidable as an industry term in the conflict section; prefer "referral agency" throughout, which is also the term the Georgia statute uses.
14. **Do not mention PACE.** Not relevant here, and the expanded name contains a banned substring.
15. **Title Case on all headings.** ECM house style.

## Couldn't find or couldn't verify

- **A 50-state survey of care manager title regulation.** No authority publishes one. The Tax Adviser's flat statement is the strongest sourcing available. This is why the post should say "no national license and no governmental body regulates the title" rather than counting states.
- **Current status of NASW's C-SWCM and C-ASWCM.** socialworkers.org would not load for me. ALCA lists both as approved, which implies they are current, but I did not confirm from NASW. Name them, describe them, claim nothing about availability.
- **Whether NACCM's NCCA accreditation is currently active.** The announcement I found carries no readable date.
- **Typical caseload size for a care manager.** No authority publishes a benchmark. The interview question is still worth asking, but the post must not supply a "good" number. Frame it as: ask, and ask who covers when they are away.
- **A sourced initial-assessment flat fee.** Only AARP's 4-to-6-hours-of-billable-time framing is defensible.
- **ALCA's Code of Ethics in full.** The PDF is member-gated and the whole domain 403s automated requests. Every ethics clause in Finding 6 came from search extracts of ALCA's own pages and PDF. **The analyzer should treat these as high-confidence-but-single-channel and paraphrase rather than quote.**
- **Independent data on how often care managers take referral fees.** No study found. The post must ask the question, not quantify the problem.
- **Confirmation of disclosure statutes in California, Oregon, Colorado, Arizona, and Maryland.** Reported in secondary coverage only. Name Georgia and Washington; say "a growing number of states" for the rest.
- **NIA and AARP pages could not be fetched directly** (405 and, for NIA, consistently blocked). AARP's content came through cleanly on one fetch; NIA's came from two agreeing independent search extractions. Both are high confidence on substance. The publisher should still verify the AARP URL resolves before linking it.
