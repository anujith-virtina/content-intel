## 2. MEDICARE

### 2a. The only true Medicare respite benefit: hospice inpatient respite

Corroborated three ways: NCOA "Does Medicare Pay for Respite Care?" (12 December 2024, fetched today), ARCH National Respite Network "How to Pay for Respite" (fetched today), and the CMS hospice levels-of-care page fetched on 2026-09-08 in the prior ECM pass. **cms.gov returned HTTP 403 to every request today**, including the hospice page that worked yesterday, so CMS is a plain-text citation only.

Mechanics, all three sources agreeing:

- **Requires hospice election.** The parent must have elected the Medicare hospice benefit under Part A, which requires physician certification of a terminal illness with a life expectancy of six months or less. No hospice election, no benefit.
- **Up to five consecutive days at a time.** Not five days a year. It resets and can be used more than once across hospice benefit periods.
- **Benefit periods:** two 90-day periods, then an unlimited number of 60-day periods (NCOA).
- **Setting:** a Medicare-certified hospital, skilled nursing facility, or inpatient hospice setting (NCOA). Respite under this benefit is **not** delivered at home.
- **Coinsurance:** 5% of the Medicare-approved amount for the respite stay, and the copayment cannot exceed that year's inpatient hospital deductible.
- **Purpose stated in the benefit itself:** relief for the family caregiver.

**Voice note for the creator:** `voice.md` bans "facility". The prior post rendered the CMS wording as "an approved inpatient setting". Reuse that solution.

### 2b. What Medicare does NOT do

Original Medicare does not cover respite care outside the hospice benefit (NCOA, 12 December 2024). It will not pay an aide to sit with your parent so you can sleep, and it will not pay for an adult day program as respite. This is the site's single most important factual guardrail and it must be stated plainly and early.

### 2c. THE FIND: the CMS GUIDE Model. Medicare respite money outside hospice.

This is the strongest and most under-published fact in the whole file. It is real, it is Medicare, it is not hospice, and it pays for respite.

**Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE)** is a CMS Innovation Center model. Verified across four approved sources:

| Fact | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Respite benefit | **Up to $2,500 a year** for respite services | AARP, updated 6 October 2025; NCOA, 14 June 2024; ARCH |
| Respite settings | The home, an adult day center, or a setting providing 24-hour care | ARCH; AARP |
| Started | July 2024, an eight-year pilot | Alzheimer's Association; NCOA |
| Cost to the family | Free. GUIDE services carry no charge to eligible participants | Alzheimer's Association |
| Scale | **330 of 390 approved programs operating, across 47 states**, excluding Alaska and the Dakotas | AARP, 6 October 2025 |
| Other caregiver support | A named care navigator, caregiver training, a 24/7 support line | AARP; Alzheimer's Association |

**Eligibility** (Alzheimer's Association and AARP agree):

- A diagnosis of Alzheimer's or another dementia. AARP specifies moderate to severe dementia, not mild cognitive impairment.
- Enrolled in **Original Medicare Parts A and B**. Medicare Advantage enrollees are excluded.
- **Not** enrolled in hospice, **not** in PACE, and **not** living in a long-term nursing home.

**The mechanic that matters most, and that competitors miss entirely:** a family **cannot self-enroll**. You cannot call Medicare and ask for GUIDE. The parent must be a patient of a participating provider organisation. NCOA's instruction is the actionable one: call the parent's primary care provider and ask whether they participate in GUIDE, then ask the provider organisation for the respite benefit.

**Sourcing caution.** The `$2,563` figure for performance year 2025 appeared only in a search summary of a CMS MLN fact sheet PDF I could not fetch (403). **`[unverified]` — do not publish $2,563.** Publish "up to $2,500 a year", attributed to AARP with its October 2025 date, which is the cleanest dated approved citation available.

### 2d. Medicare Advantage

Some Advantage plans add supplemental benefits Original Medicare does not cover, and NCOA names in-home respite and adult day services among them (12 December 2024). **Must be hedged.** No claim that any specific plan covers respite. Safe instruction: check the plan's evidence of coverage, or call the number on the card.

## 3. MEDICAID

**Blocking constraint: no asset limit, no income cap, no look-back period, no spend-down figure, anywhere in this post.** Route every eligibility question to an elder law attorney. This section explains *mechanisms*, not *qualification*.

### 3a. 1915(c) HCBS waivers

Respite is one of the services a state may cover under a Medicaid 1915(c) home and community based services waiver. medicaid.gov returned HTTP 403 today, as it did on 2026-09-08, so this is a plain-text citation to Medicaid.gov and CMS, never a link.

ARCH states that generally every state offers some respite assistance through Medicaid, with the amount and the rules set state by state, and points families to the CMS state waiver list and their own state Medicaid office. Waivers may cap the number of people served, which is why interest lists and waiting lists exist. Say "waiting lists are common in many states" rather than quantifying.

### 3b. 1915(i) state plan HCBS. A route most families have never heard of.

ARCH lists **23 states** offering home and community based services through a 1915(i) state plan option, where eligibility is based on **state-defined functional need** rather than an institutional level of care. ARCH's list: Arkansas, California, Connecticut, Delaware, DC, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Louisiana, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Nevada, New Hampshire, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin.

**Recommendation: name the mechanism, do not publish the 23-state list.** The list has no date on the ARCH page and state lists rot fast. One sentence: some states cover home and community based services through the Medicaid state plan rather than a waiver, so ask your state Medicaid office about both routes.

### 3c. Self-direction, and paying a family member

Verified from ARCH "How Do I Get Paid to be a Family Caregiver?" (fetched today):

- Self-directed or consumer-directed programs, sometimes called cash and counseling, let a Medicaid-eligible person control a budget and choose their own workers.
- Some states require the family caregiver to become a certified Medicaid provider first.
- **Structured Family Caregiving** exists as a Medicaid benefit in seven states named by ARCH (Connecticut, Georgia, Indiana, Louisiana, Missouri, North Carolina, South Dakota), for people needing 24-hour care.
- **Adult foster care** in seven states named by ARCH allows relatives as paid providers. ARCH notes spouses are excluded in most states.
- ARCH's finding tool is Applied Self-Direction, which is **not on ECM's approved source list**. Route the reader to their state Medicaid office and their ADRC instead.

**Why this belongs in a "who pays" post:** self-direction is how a family converts a Medicaid benefit into paid hours delivered by someone they already trust, which is the trust barrier answered with money.

### 3d. CONFLICT on self-direction coverage

- **ARCH:** every state except South Dakota has Medicaid programs offering self-directed services.
- **KFF, 2025 (not on ECM's approved list):** all reporting states except Alaska allow enrollees to self-direct in at least some circumstances.

The two name different exception states. **Unresolved. Publish neither.** Say "most states offer some form of self-direction, and the rules differ in every one".

### 3e. PACE

**BANNED SUBSTRING WARNING.** The official federal program name contains "the Elderly" and the banned-substring check will block the push. **Never write the expanded name.** Use the acronym PACE and define it functionally.

Verified from NCOA, "Program of All-Inclusive Care" article, published 10 December 2025:

- Age 55 or older, living in the service area of a PACE program, certified by the state as needing nursing home level care, and able to live safely in the community with PACE support.
- Neither Medicare nor Medicaid enrollment is strictly required. Someone with Medicare but not Medicaid pays a monthly premium for the long-term care portion plus a Part D premium. Someone with Medicaid pays a small monthly amount or nothing.
- **Respite:** the NCOA article I fetched does **not** list respite among covered services. A separate NCOA search summary and other secondary material do describe PACE as including respite and caregiver support. **`[unverified]` on respite specifically.**

**Recommended handling:** describe PACE functionally as a combined Medicare and Medicaid program for older adults who qualify for nursing home level care but want to stay home, note that the model is built around a day health center that gives the family caregiver regular daytime hours, and say that whether formal respite is part of the care plan is a question for the local PACE program. Do not assert a respite benefit.

## 4. THE OLDER AMERICANS ACT: NFCSP. The one most families never hear about.

**Title III-E of the Older Americans Act, the National Family Caregiver Support Program**, established in 2000. ACL awards grants to states and territories based on their share of the population aged 70 and over. Money flows to state units on aging, then to **Area Agencies on Aging**, which deliver services locally.

**acl.gov returned HTTP 403 today**, as it did on 2026-09-08. ACL is a plain-text citation only. Program facts below are corroborated by ARCH (fetched today) and the ACL text indexed in search.

**Five funded service categories, with respite named explicitly:**

1. Information about available services
2. Assistance gaining access to services
3. Counseling, support groups, and caregiver training
4. **Respite care**
5. Supplemental services on a limited basis

**Eligibility, per ARCH (fetched today):**

- Caring for someone aged 60 or over, **or**
- Caring for someone of any age living with Alzheimer's or a related dementia, **or**
- A grandparent aged 55 or over raising a grandchild, **or**
- A relative aged 55 or over caring for an adult child with a disability

**The application route is the whole point.** You do not apply to NFCSP. You call your **Area Agency on Aging**, and you find it through the **Eldercare Locator**, 1-800-677-1116 or eldercare.acl.gov, a public service of the Administration for Community Living. ARCH gives exactly this instruction.

### Cost sharing: handle carefully

A search summary attributed to AARP's Policy Book and ACL fiscal regulations states that the Older Americans Act permits voluntary contributions without coercion or a means test, and that states enacting cost sharing should exempt people below a stated share of the federal poverty level. **`[unverified]`, and the poverty-level figure is an eligibility figure.** Do not publish either the "no income test" claim or the percentage.

**Safe phrasing:** NFCSP-funded respite is not means tested the way Medicaid is, but availability, waiting times, and any request for a voluntary contribution vary by Area Agency on Aging. Ask yours directly.

## 5. LIFESPAN RESPITE CARE PROGRAM AND STATE RESPITE NETWORKS

A federal ACL grant program that funds states to build coordinated respite systems across all ages and conditions instead of program-by-program silos. Grantees are also expected to develop emergency respite and to recruit and train respite workers.

Verified today from ARCH's Lifespan Respite program page: a funding announcement released **22 June 2026** with **10 expected awards**, two application tracks (new or restarting programs, and enhancement grants), open to all states, DC, and the territories, with an application deadline of 22 July 2026.

**What it means for a family, which is what the post should carry:**

- Not every state has a Lifespan Respite Care Program (ARCH says so explicitly).
- Where they exist, they commonly offer **respite vouchers, grants, or stipends**, often through self-directed arrangements.
- ARCH says these are typically aimed at families who do **not** qualify for other publicly funded programs. That is the important placement: this is the payer of last resort before private pay.
- Route: your **state respite coalition** or state Lifespan Respite program, reachable through ARCH, or your **ADRC / No Wrong Door** system.

**Cut from the prior pass and still cut:** the "39 states and DC since 2009" figure and the "$10 million FY 2025 appropriation" figure. Both came from indexed ACL text, acl.gov 403s, and neither adds anything for the reader.

### ARCH National Respite Locator

Verified today. A free, searchable national database operated by ARCH, listing mainly home care agencies, assisted living communities, and state or community-based agencies that provide respite. ARCH states plainly that it is **not a complete list**, does not endorse providers, and that vetting is the family's job. Website support is acknowledged as 75% federal funding from ACL ($1,842,417) with 25% non-federal.

**Editorial value:** the honesty about incompleteness and non-endorsement is useful and on-brand. It also sits well beside ECM's own directory without competing with it.

## 6. VA

All facts below fetched from VA.gov today. ECM's `/veterans-benefits/` path resolves to a single blog post, not a directory, so do **not** use it with a directory anchor. Link post 259250 instead.

### 6a. The general VA respite benefit

- Available to enrolled veterans who meet clinical criteria, delivered in the home, through adult day health programs, or in a VA community living center or contracted community nursing setting.
- **Ordinarily limited to no more than 30 days per year** (ARCH, and the VA respite fact sheet verified 2026-09-08).
- A **copay may apply**, based on service-connected status and financial information, worked out with a VA social worker using VA Form 10-10EC.
- **Route:** the veteran's VA social worker or case manager.

**Still unresolved, still do not publish:** how the VA counts a respite day. Sources contradicted each other on 2026-09-08 (6 hours at home versus a 24-hour period) and I found nothing today to resolve it. Say the day count differs by setting and route to the VA social worker.

### 6b. PCAFC. Now verified on VA.gov, not a search summary.

Fetched today from the VA.gov PCAFC page. The prior pass flagged the respite figure as `[unverified]`. **It is now verified.**

**Veteran must meet all of:** a VA disability rating, individual or combined, of **70% or higher**; discharged from the military or with a medical discharge date; needing **at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care**; enrolled in VA health care.

**Caregiver must be:** at least 18, and either related to the veteran or living with them full time.

**Primary Family Caregiver benefits, as listed by VA:** a monthly stipend, health care through CHAMPVA, free legal and financial planning assistance, **at least 30 days of respite care per year**, telehealth therapy, commissary and exchange access, education, training, and mental health counseling.

**Secondary Family Caregivers** receive training, mental health counseling, and travel benefits, but not the stipend.

**How to apply:** online, by mail using **VA Form 10-10CG**, or in person at a VA medical center. **The veteran and the caregiver apply together.**

### 6c. PGCSS. The one with no application form.

Fetched today from caregiver.va.gov. The Program of General Caregiver Support Services covers anyone providing personal care to a veteran enrolled in VA health care. **The caregiver does not need to be a relative and does not need to live with the veteran.**

Services include skills training, coaching, peer support mentoring, telephone and online support, and **respite care described as temporary paid care so the caregiver can take a break**.

**The mechanic worth highlighting:** there is **no formal application**. You contact your local Caregiver Support Coordinator, complete an intake, the veteran confirms you as their caregiver, and you start using services. PGCSS is the fallback when a family does not clear the 70% rating bar for PCAFC, and almost no consumer article says so.

### 6d. Veteran-Directed Care

Fetched today from VA.gov. The veteran or their representative receives a budget and works with a counselor on a spending plan, and **may hire their own family member or neighbor** for personal care and daily living help. Available to enrolled veterans who are eligible for community care, meet clinical criteria, and live where the program operates.

**The VA page does not say whether respite can be purchased with the budget.** `[unverified]`. Frame it as a way to pay for regular in-home hours, which functions as respite, and tell the reader to ask the VA social worker what the budget may cover.

## 7. LONG-TERM CARE INSURANCE

Source: NCOA, "Long-Term Care Insurance: Costs, Coverage, and How It Works", published **21 August 2026**, fetched today. Very current, approved, and dated.

- **Benefit trigger:** the policy pays when the person has cognitive impairment, or trouble with **two or more activities of daily living** (bathing, dressing, eating, grooming, toileting, moving around).
- **Elimination period:** a waiting period after the trigger is approved, which NCOA describes as roughly one to three months, during which the family pays for care themselves. It works like a deductible measured in days.
- **Respite care is named among covered services**, alongside in-home care, assisted living, adult day services, memory care, and nursing home care.
- **Payment structure:** most policies pay up to a daily limit until the policy's lifetime maximum is reached.

**Why families leave this money unclaimed, framed honestly:** the benefit only starts after a trigger is certified and an elimination period runs, so a family who buys four hours a week of respite and never files is never counted as being in the elimination period at all. The practical advice is to notify the insurer early and in writing, and to confirm before booking whether the chosen provider meets the policy's licensing requirements.

**`[unverified]`, do not publish:** any claim that policies commonly waive the elimination period for respite, or that respite carries its own separate day limit. Both are plausible and both appear in industry material, but no approved source states either. Replace with: ask the insurer to confirm in writing what the policy pays for respite, how many days, and whether the elimination period applies.

## 8. STATE PROGRAMS, ADRCs, GRANTS, AND SLIDING SCALES

From ARCH "How to Pay for Respite" (fetched today) unless noted.

- **State family caregiver support and respite programs** exist outside Medicaid in many states and vary widely. ARCH points families to Family Caregiver Alliance's state services resource. FCA is on ECM's approved list; the specific FCA navigator URL I tried returned 404, so name FCA in plain text rather than linking a URL I could not verify.
- **ADRC / No Wrong Door** is the single most useful phrase in this section and almost nobody uses it in consumer content. ARCH routes families to their local Aging and Disability Resource Center through eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116, the same number as the Eldercare Locator. One phone number reaches the AAA, the ADRC, and NFCSP-funded respite.
- **Sliding fee scales and scholarships:** ARCH notes some adult day centers offer need-based scholarships or income-based fees. Ask.
- **Disease-specific grants:** ARCH names the Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration's Comstock Respite Grant, HFC's caregiver respite program, Alzheimer's Association local chapters, and NORD's rare disease grants of up to $500 a year. NCOA (14 June 2024) separately names Hilarity for Charity, which offers three to six months of in-home care to dementia caregivers, and disease-specific respite grants from organisations including the Multiple Sclerosis Foundation.
- **Faith-based and volunteer networks:** ARCH names Lutheran Services in America for no-cost or low-cost home and community respite, and the Respite For All Foundation's volunteer-staffed day respite model for people in early to middle stage dementia, at a nominal fee or on scholarship.
- **Military families outside VA:** TRICARE's Extended Care Health Option and the Exceptional Family Member Program both include respite (ARCH). Worth one clause, since ECM has a veteran audience.

**Naming caution:** `brand.md` bans recommending a specific named provider, community, or firm. Naming a national nonprofit grant program is not the same as recommending a provider, but keep it to a short list and frame it as "ask about", not "apply here". Recommend naming at most two.

## 9. EMPLOYER BENEFITS, FMLA, AND TAX

### FMLA does not pay for respite, and the post should say so plainly

Source: AARP, "How Caregivers Can Use FMLA to Care for a Family Member", updated **5 September 2024**, fetched today.

- **12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave** in a 12-month period. Health insurance continues. 26 weeks for military caregiver leave.
- **Eligibility:** 12 or more months with the employer, at least 1,250 hours in the past 12 months, and an employer with 50 or more employees within 75 miles.
- **Leave can be intermittent** or taken as a reduced schedule, which is the detail that matters here: a caregiver can take regular hours off rather than one long block.
- Covered relatives include a parent, spouse, and child. **In-laws, siblings, and grandparents are generally not covered.**
- AARP notes FMLA does not cover 44% of US workers, and that some states, cities, and employers go beyond the federal minimum with paid leave.

**Editorial framing:** FMLA is not a funding source. It buys you time, not care. Include it as the honest answer to "my employer, then?" and keep it to two or three sentences.

### Employer caregiving benefits

Weakly sourced. Backup adult care, caregiving stipends, lifestyle spending accounts, and dependent care FSAs all appear in employer-benefits material, but I found **no approved source** documenting prevalence or mechanics. **Recommendation: one sentence, no statistics.** Tell the reader to ask HR whether the employer offers backup adult care or a dependent care account, because it costs nothing to ask.

### Tax

**Do not explain any credit, deduction, or account.** NCOA's own financial assistance article routes readers to an accountant on every tax-adjacent item. One sentence: whether any of this is deductible is a question for a tax professional.
