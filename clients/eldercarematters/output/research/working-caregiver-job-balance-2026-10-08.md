## Sub-questions a reader would want answered

1. Does FMLA actually apply to me, and what does it get me if it does?
2. How do I even start the conversation with my manager or HR without risking my job?
3. What can I ask for short of unpaid leave (flexible hours, remote days, EAP)?
4. At what point does trying to do it all myself cost more than hiring help?
5. What happens if my employer is too small for FMLA to apply?

## Key findings

### Finding 1: FMLA gives job protection, not income

- Source: [Family Caregivers: Information on the Family and Medical Leave Act](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fmla/family-caregiver) — U.S. Department of Labor, fetched 2026-10-08
- What it says: Eligible employees get 12 workweeks of leave in a 12-month period to care for a parent
  with a serious health condition; the leave is unpaid but job-protected.
- Why it matters: Most working caregivers conflate "I can take leave" with "I can afford to take leave."
  ECM content should separate those two facts clearly up front.

### Finding 2: Eligibility has three real gates, and competitors skip them

- Source: paraphrased from general FMLA statute requirements cited across multiple secondary sources
  (WebMD, Griswold) during this research pass
- What it says: FMLA only applies at employers with 50+ employees, to staff employed at least 12 months
  who worked at least 1,250 hours in the prior year.
- Why it matters: Two of the five competitor articles fetched in full skip these thresholds entirely,
  leaving readers who work for a small employer believing they are covered when they are not.

### Finding 3: FMLA leave does not have to be taken all at once

- Source: general FMLA provision, confirmed across DOL framing and secondary sources
- What it says: Intermittent leave is allowed, so a caregiver can use single days or partial weeks rather
  than one continuous block.
- Why it matters: This is the single most useful fact for a working caregiver trying to keep a job while
  managing ongoing (not acute) parent care, and it is underused in competitor content, which tends to
  frame FMLA as an all-or-nothing block.

### Finding 4: Six in ten family caregivers of people 50+ also hold a job

- Source: cited in WebMD's [How To Balance Caregiving and Work](https://www.webmd.com/healthy-aging/caregiver-balance-work) (secondary source, figure traced to Caregiving in the US data, not independently re-verified in this pass) `[unverified]`
- What it says: A majority of family caregivers are simultaneously employed.
- Why it matters: Flags this is a mainstream, not fringe, reader situation. **Flagged unverified** because
  the original survey source was not independently located during this research pass; the creator should
  either verify this against the National Alliance for Caregiving/AARP "Caregiving in the US" report
  directly, name that source, or cut the figure per the no-invented-statistics rule.

### Finding 5: State paid family leave programs exist but are not universal

- Source: [How California Caregiver Laws Are Changing in 2026](https://www.caregivercalifornia.org/2026/02/10/how-california-caregiver-laws-are-changing-in-2026-and-what-it-means-for-you/) — California Caregiver Resource Centers
- What it says: California's Paid Family Leave program is expanding wage replacement and job-reinstatement
  rights in 2026.
- Why it matters: This is state-specific and will change again. Per the YMYL rule on state-variable
  figures, the post should name which states have a program (California, New Jersey, New York, Washington,
  Colorado, Massachusetts) without stating a dollar amount or percentage, and route the reader to check
  their own state's labor department.

### Finding 6: The employer conversation works best as a planned meeting, not a hallway chat

- Source: [How to Talk to Your Employer About Your Caregiving Responsibilities](https://theconversationproject.org/tcp-blog/employee-caregiver-resources/) — The Conversation Project
- What it says: A four-step framework: clarify needs, plan the approach, schedule a deliberate meeting,
  and explicitly ask about FMLA, paid leave, and flexible arrangements.
- Why it matters: Gives ECM's post a concrete, actionable structure the reader can literally follow before
  a real conversation, matching the "take one concrete next action" requirement in audience.md.

## Conflicts and disagreements

- **Position A** (Griswold Home Care): frames FMLA as an alternative to hiring paid in-home help, "use
  the leave instead of hiring someone."
- **Position B** (WebMD, The Conversation Project): frames FMLA and professional help as complementary,
  something to combine with outside support rather than a replacement for it.
- **What's actually true:** Position B fits ECM's directory-first business model better and is also the
  more financially realistic framing, since unpaid leave has a real income cost that hiring part-time
  help may not exceed. **This is the recommended framing for the brief.**

## The gap

> No competitor piece treats "protect your job" and "get professional caregiving help" as one decision.
> They are presented as separate, unconnected options, and none give the reader a way to weigh the real
> cost of unpaid leave against a few hours a week of paid support.

## Recommended angle

> Working caregivers do not have to choose between a job and a parent. FMLA protects the job; a home care
> aide or geriatric care manager protects the hours. Treat both as one plan.
