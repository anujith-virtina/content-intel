## Key findings

### Finding 1: Falls are the trigger event, and the CDC numbers are large and current

- Source: [Facts About Falls](https://www.cdc.gov/falls/data-research/facts-stats/index.html) — CDC, figures drawn from WISQARS, page cites data accessed 25 August 2026
- What it says: More than one in four adults 65 and older falls each year. About 4.5 million emergency department visits a year follow older-adult falls, of which about 3.1 million are treated and released and about 1.4 million become hospitalizations. Roughly 319,000 older adults are hospitalized for hip fractures annually. About 37 percent of those who fall report an injury needing medical treatment or restricting activity for at least a day (Moreland et al., 2020). Less than half of people who fall tell their doctor.
- Why it matters: This is the only statistic block in the post. It is CDC, dated, and traceable. It justifies the whole category without fear-mongering.
- **Note for creator:** the older "3 million ED visits" figure that circulates widely is out of date. Use 4.5 million and name CDC.

### Finding 2: The strongest argument for a medical alert system is not the fall, it is the time on the floor afterwards

- Source: Fleming and Brayne, [Inability to get up after falling, subsequent time on floor, and summoning help](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2590903/) — BMJ, 2008, UK prospective cohort, 110 participants, all over 90, median age 94
- What it says: 80 percent (53 of 66) were unable to get up unaided after at least one fall. 30 percent (20 of 66) lay on the floor for an hour or more. 82 percent of falls (217 of 265) happened when the person was alone. Long time on the floor was strongly associated with serious injury, hospital admission, and a later move into long-term care.
- **The devastating detail:** the authors report that call alarms were widely available but were not used in most of the falls that ended in a long lie.
- Why it matters: This is the honest spine of the entire post. The device only helps if it is worn and pressed. Everything else in the buying decision follows from that one sentence.
- **Limitation the creator must state:** small UK cohort of people over 90. Frame it as research on the very old, not as a US national figure. Do not extrapolate to all older adults.

### Finding 3: Automatic fall detection is genuinely unreliable, and this is the honesty item nobody in the SERP will print

- Source: Bagalà et al., [Evaluation of Accelerometer-Based Fall Detection Algorithms on Real-World Falls](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0037062) — PLOS ONE, 2012. 29 real falls from 15 people, mean age 66.
- What it says: Algorithms that report near-perfect results in the lab averaged 57.0 percent sensitivity (SD 27.3, best 82.8 percent) on real falls, with false alarms ranging from 3 to 85 per 24 hours of monitoring.
- Corroborating review: Pech et al., [Falls Detection and Prevention Systems in Home Care for Older Adults: Myth or Reality?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8704100/) — JMIR Aging, 2021. Same conclusion: controlled-setting accuracy does not survive contact with real homes. This review also reports a finding that 80 percent of frail older adults equipped with an emergency system forgot to press the button after a fall. `[unverified]` — the review cites this to an underlying study I did not open. Treat as supporting colour, not as a headline number.
- A widely repeated figure that one device's manufacturer-reported sensitivity of about 94 percent fell to 25 percent on real-world falls appears in secondary summaries. `[unverified]` — I could not trace it to the primary paper. **Do not publish it.**
- Why it matters: Fall detection is sold as the premium upgrade and is almost always a paid add-on. The defensible, sourced line is: peer-reviewed testing on real falls shows detection rates well below the marketing claims, so treat it as a backup to pressing the button, never a replacement. That sentence is worth the whole article.

### Finding 4: Original Medicare does not pay. This is the YMYL centre of gravity.

- Primary source status: **medicare.gov returned HTTP 403 to my request**, as MUST-FOLLOW-RULES section 3 predicted. I could not read or verify a medicare.gov URL. Name Medicare in plain text, link nothing.
- Corroborating approved source: NCOA, [Does Insurance Cover Medical Alert Systems?](https://www.ncoa.org/article/does-insurance-cover-medical-alert-systems/), dated 4 February 2026. States plainly that Original Medicare Parts A and B do not pay the costs of medical alert systems. Also: most Medicaid programs do not cover them and coverage varies by state; most private insurance does not cover them; some long-term care policies do, subject to policy terms.
- Mechanism, not eligibility: Original Medicare Part B pays for durable medical equipment. A medical alert system is not classified as durable medical equipment, which is why it falls outside Part B. State the mechanism, not a coverage promise.
- Medicare Advantage: plans may offer supplemental benefits beyond Original Medicare. The category of allowable supplemental benefits was broadened in 2018 to include non-medical in-home supports, and plans choose individually which to offer, with their own cost sharing and limits ([42 CFR 422.102](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-422/subpart-C/section-422.102), eCFR). So the only correct instruction is: read the plan's Evidence of Coverage, or call the number on the card, and ask specifically about personal emergency response.
- Why it matters: this is the number one search-adjacent question and the number one place a YMYL post gets it wrong.

### Finding 5: Medicaid may cover it, but only as PERS under a home and community based services waiver, and only in some states

- Source: [12VAC30-122-470, Personal emergency response system service](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title12/agency30/chapter122/section470/) — Virginia Administrative Code. A real state Medicaid regulation, used here purely to show the mechanics.
- What it says: PERS is defined as an electronic device plus a monitoring service that lets a person summon help. The functional test is living alone, or being alone for significant parts of the day without a caregiver, and otherwise needing supervision. The reimbursed service is a one-time installation (including account activation, instruction for the person and the caregiver, and later removal of equipment) plus a monthly rental. The device must have a waterproof wireless activator that can be worn, must transmit a low-battery signal, must allow hands-free two-way voice with the response center, and must seize the phone line ahead of normal use. It must be FCC approved and meet UL 1635 and UL 1637. The provider must staff trained operators who receive signals 24 hours a day, every day of the year, determine whether an emergency exists, and notify an emergency response organization. Malfunctioning equipment must be repaired or replaced within 24 hours, and the provider must keep backup monitoring capacity.
- Why it matters twice over: (1) it shows readers that Medicaid PERS coverage exists as a waiver service, without stating any eligibility rule; (2) **a state Medicaid regulation is effectively a free minimum specification sheet** that any family can use when shopping privately. That is a genuinely new angle.
- **YMYL guardrail:** name it as Virginia's rule, say waiver services and their availability vary by state, and route eligibility to an elder law attorney or the state Medicaid agency. **No asset limit, no income cap, no look-back period anywhere in the post.**

### Finding 6: The money is layered, and the monthly fee is the smallest part of the trap

- Source: NCOA, [How Much Do Medical Alert Systems Actually Cost?](https://www.ncoa.org/product-resources/medical-alert-systems/medical-alert-systems-cost/), published 5 January 2026. Category-level figures, drawn from NCOA's survey of more than 1,000 medical alert system users and caregivers.
- Monthly monitoring: roughly 20 to 60 dollars, with an average around 40 dollars. Basic landline service sits at the bottom of that range.
- Fall detection add-on: about 10 to 11 dollars a month on top.
- Equipment: 0 to 200 dollars, and **most providers lease rather than sell**, so the equipment goes back when service ends.
- Activation or installation: 25 to 100 dollars one time, though not every provider charges it.
- Cancellation: most providers do not penalise cancellation, but some require a three-month minimum and some charge restocking fees of up to 50 dollars.
- Annual all-in: NCOA puts in-home landline service at roughly 275 to 485 dollars a year and mobile service at roughly 384 to 519 dollars a year, before fall detection and before promotions.
- NCOA also reports, from its **February 2025 Medical Alerts Survey**, that 75 percent of buyers bought after they or someone they knew had fallen.
- Why it matters: this is the only place in the post where dollar figures appear, and every one of them can be attributed to NCOA with a date, satisfying the YMYL cost rule.
- **Flag for analyzer:** NCOA's product-resources pages carry an explicit affiliate disclosure ("supported in part by commissions earned from purchases made through shared affiliate links"). NCOA is on ECM's approved source list and the category-level cost data is the best dated data available, but the analyzer should decide whether to cite NCOA in body text, link it, or cite in text only without a link. **My recommendation: name NCOA and the date in the body text, do not spend one of the two external link slots on it.**

### Finding 7: "Professionally monitored" and the certifications behind it mean something specific, and less than the marketing implies

- Source: [Five Diamond Designation](https://tma.us/programs/fivediamond/) — The Monitoring Association (TMA), the industry body.
- What it says: Five Diamond is awarded annually to **monitoring centers**, not to devices. The five commitments are: 100 percent of operators certified through TMA's training and recertified every three years; submission to random inspections against quality criteria by a nationally recognised testing laboratory such as UL, FM Approvals, or Intertek/ETL; a customer service commitment; TMA membership and participation; and a commitment to reducing false dispatches. Operators must demonstrate alarm verification, communication with 911 public safety answering points, and emergency preparedness across scenarios.
- UL, separately: UL 827 covers the central station itself and UL 1981 covers its automation systems ([Intertek summary of UL 1981](https://www.intertek.com/life-safety-security/standards/ul-1981/)). UL 1637 is the standard for home health care signaling equipment, which is the device end of a PERS ([UL standards catalog entry](https://standardscatalog.ul.com/standards/en/standard_1637_5)).
- Why it matters: a family can ask two precise questions instead of accepting the word "certified". Is the monitoring center itself UL listed, and is it TMA Five Diamond, and for what year. **Honesty item:** none of these certify response speed to your parent, and Five Diamond expires after one year, so "certified" without a year is meaningless.
- **Do not overstate.** Five Diamond is a designation from a trade association, awarded on commitments and third-party inspection, not a government safety approval.

### Finding 8: The phone line and the power supply are the two silent failure points

- Source: FCC consumer guidance on [VoIP and 911 Service](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/voip-and-911-service) and the [VoIP consumer guide](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/voice-over-internet-protocol-voip).
- What it says: digital and internet-based phone service may not be compatible with alarm systems that dial a monitoring station, and an alarm may fail to reach the center. VoIP equipment and the internet modem both need battery backup, because VoIP is not required to carry backup power. Alarm systems may not detect that the internet connection has dropped, so a household can believe it is protected when the link to the central station is already gone. Users are advised to test transmission regularly.
- Why it matters: enormous numbers of older adults have been moved off copper onto cable or fibre phone service without anyone re-testing the alert system. This is a concrete, sourced, actionable check that the affiliate reviews do not make.
- Pair it with the Virginia rule's low-battery-signal and 24-hour-repair requirements, and with the backup battery question for the base station during a power cut.

### Finding 9: There is a documented federal enforcement history of "free medical alert" robocall fraud

- Sources: FTC, [Settlement stops operations that used robocalls to fraudulently pitch medical alert devices](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2014/11/settlement-ftc-florida-attorney-general-stops-operations-used-robocalls-fraudulently-pitch-medical) (November 2014); FTC, [Refunds totaling more than $1.8 million to consumers defrauded by Lifewatch, Inc.](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/12/ftc-issues-refunds-totaling-more-18-million-consumers-defrauded-lifewatch-incs-deceptive-medical) (1 December 2021).
- What they say: telemarketers used prerecorded calls to tell older adults that a medical alert system had already been bought for them by a relative or friend, and that there would be no cost. They falsely implied endorsement by reputable health organisations, including the American Heart Association. Victims were then billed monthly monitoring fees and hit with cancellation penalties. In the Lifewatch matter the FTC mailed 71,899 refund checks totalling more than 1.8 million dollars, averaging about 25 dollars each. FTC materials describe the operation as involving more than a billion unsolicited robocalls.
- FTC's standing consumer advice: an unsolicited prerecorded sales call you never consented to is illegal. Do not press a number, do not talk to the live operator, and never give a bank account, card, or Medicare number on such a call.
- Why it matters: the tell is unmistakable and easy to teach. **No legitimate provider will tell you a relative already bought it and refuse to say who.** That sentence belongs in the post.
- **Brand flag:** Lifewatch, Inc. is named here as an FTC enforcement defendant. See the brand mention register below.

### Finding 10: The pendant is the wrong tool for a parent who will not wear it or cannot press it

- Sources: Alzheimer's Association, [Technology Safety for Older Adults](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/safety/technology-safety-older-adults); AARP, [How to Choose a Medical Alert or Monitoring System](https://www.aarp.org/caregiving/home-care/medic-alert-systems-options/), updated 29 May 2026.
- Alzheimer's Association: no location tracking system is fully accurate, some devices work poorly indoors, some can only place a person in a general vicinity, and weather can interfere. Their own framing, well under 15 words: **"Location tracking is not the same as having a 24-hour companion."** Location tracking is one measure among several, not a standalone solution.
- AARP describes the wider category: wearables (pendant, wristband, smartwatch), fixed wall or table help buttons for the bathroom and kitchen, hubs with two-way speakers, smart speakers, passive fall detection using radar or infrared with nothing worn at all, motion and door and bed sensors, video monitoring, and in-car devices with crash detection. AARP flags waterproofing as vital because so many falls happen in the bathroom and kitchen, and notes charging time matters because nights are high-risk. AARP's article carries a 14-question pre-purchase checklist covering waterproofing, comfort, activation methods, battery, power source, privacy, response center credentials, customer service hours, false alarm policy, cancellation, reputation, pricing transparency, and a caregiver app.
- AARP also notes veterans should ask their VA primary care provider about eligibility for a device.
- Why it matters: this is the honest counter-recommendation. If the parent has moderate dementia and will not wear a pendant or will not understand pressing it, the answer may be passive sensors, a wall button, or in-home help, not a better pendant. A directory site can say that. An affiliate site cannot.

### Finding 11: False alarms are the norm, not the exception, and families are unprepared for what happens

- Source: Consumer Reports, [How to Handle Accidental Activations and Other Issues With Medical Alert Devices](https://www.consumerreports.org/health/medical-alert-systems/how-to-handle-issues-with-medical-alert-devices-a1085568543/). Survey of 1,869 CR members. `[unverified]` — the survey year was not stated on the page I read; the analyzer should either find the year or drop the percentage and keep the qualitative point.
- What it says: 62 percent of respondents had accidentally activated the device, and 14 percent reported a malfunction. Causes include pets stepping on buttons and over-sensitive fall detection. Manufacturers describe an explicit trade-off between catching real falls and generating constant false positives.
- Response protocol, which is the useful bit: on activation the center speaks to the user first, then works down a pre-programmed contact list, and dispatches responders only if nobody answers. Most devices have a cancel button. Systems can be set to text or email a caregiver a summary of every interaction.
- Related CR finding: a large majority of members' devices had a portable button, fewer than half had an in-home wall or table button, and about 36 percent came with fall detection.
- Why it matters: it defuses the "will they break down the door" fear, and it turns into a concrete setup task, which is exactly what ECM's audience rule demands (one concrete next action).

## How the category is actually structured (for the comparison table)

Four buying decisions, in the order a family should make them.

**1. Where does the parent need protection?**
- **In-home only.** Base station plus a worn pendant or wristband. Works within a radius of the base. Cheapest tier.
- **Mobile / on-the-go.** Speaker, microphone, and GPS in the worn device itself, so the center can locate a person who cannot say where they are. No base station required.
- **Both.** Many providers bundle an in-home base with a mobile unit.

**2. How does the base connect?**
- **Landline.** Traditional copper. Historically the cheapest plan.
- **Cellular.** No home phone needed. Depends entirely on carrier coverage at the parent's address.
- **VoIP / cable / fibre phone.** The risk zone. See Finding 8.

**3. Monitored or not?**
- **Professionally monitored.** 24/7 center, trained operators, two-way voice, contact-list escalation, dispatch. Carries the monthly fee.
- **Unmonitored.** Device dials 911 or a family member directly. No monthly fee, but nobody assesses the situation, nobody works a contact list, and 911 gets every false alarm.

**4. What is worn?**
- Pendant, wristband, smartwatch-style device, or a fixed wall button. Plus the passive, nothing-worn option (radar or infrared sensors) for a parent who will not wear anything.

Automatic fall detection sits across all four as a paid monthly add-on.

## Questions to ask a provider before signing (draft for the post's numbered list)

Drawn from the AARP checklist, the Virginia PERS regulation's minimum specification, the FCC guidance, and the NCOA cost breakdown. Analyzer should trim to 8 to 10.

1. Is the monitoring center staffed 24 hours a day, every day of the year, by your own trained operators, or is monitoring subcontracted?
2. Is the monitoring center UL listed, and does it hold a current TMA Five Diamond designation? For which year?
3. What exactly happens when the button is pressed? Who is called first, second, third, and when do you dispatch?
4. What is the pendant-to-base range in feet, and will you help me test it in the actual house, including the far bedroom and the yard?
5. Does the device have hands-free two-way voice, or does my parent have to get back to the base station to speak to you?
6. Is the wearable waterproof or only water resistant, and can it be worn in the shower? What is the IP rating?
7. How long does the wearable's battery last, how long does it take to charge, and does the device warn you when it is low?
8. How long does the base station keep working in a power cut?
9. My parent's phone service is cable or internet based. Will your system reach you over it, and how do we test that?
10. What is the total first-year cost: monthly fee, fall detection, activation, equipment, shipping, tax?
11. Do I own the equipment or lease it, and what do I owe if we return it?
12. Is there a contract term, a minimum, an auto-renewal, a restocking fee, and are refunds prorated?
13. Is there a trial period, and can I cancel for any reason within it?
14. Is the price locked, or can the monthly fee rise?
15. Is there a caregiver app that shows battery status and whether the device is actually being worn?

The "is it being worn" question in item 15 is the sleeper. It is the only one that addresses Finding 2.

## Conflicts and disagreements

- **Fall detection: marketing versus peer review.** Vendors and affiliate reviews present fall detection as a reliable safety net. Peer-reviewed testing on real falls (Bagalà 2012, corroborated by Pech 2021) shows real-world sensitivity far below lab claims and false alarm rates high enough to matter. **Resolution:** both are true and the tension is the story. Fall detection catches some falls the person cannot report, and misses others. It is a backup, not a replacement. Say so.
- **Medicaid coverage: "may be covered" versus "most programs do not."** State waiver documents show PERS as a routinely offered HCBS waiver service. NCOA states most Medicaid programs do not cover medical alert systems. **Resolution:** not a real conflict. PERS is commonly available *within waiver programs*, but waiver programs cover a limited population, so most Medicaid enrollees do not get it. Write it as: it exists as a waiver service in many states, availability and rules vary, ask the state Medicaid agency or an elder law attorney.
- **ED visit figure.** CDC's current page says about 4.5 million; a widely repeated older figure says about 3 million, and some sources cite 3.85 million for 2023. **Resolution:** use CDC's current published figure and name CDC. Do not average them.
- **Monitored versus unmonitored.** Affiliate reviews near-universally recommend monitored. The counter-position is real: an unmonitored device with no monthly fee may be the only affordable option, and for a lucid parent with reliable local contacts it may be enough. **Resolution:** present the trade-off honestly rather than steering. ECM has no stake either way.

## Competitive scan

The SERP for "medical alert systems for seniors" is almost entirely affiliate-monetised ranked lists. Several are ECM competitors that must be scanned but never cited or linked.

1. **NCOA, "The Best Medical Alert Systems in 2026"** — ncoa.org. Angle: ranked list of six, organised by superlative (Best Overall, Best Customer Service, Best Affordable). 2. **U.S. News, "8 Best Medical Alert Systems 2026"** — usnews.com. Angle: methodology-heavy ranked list with a separate "Does Medicare Cover" explainer. **Gap:** the buying framework is subordinate to the ranking; a reader who does not want any of the eight brands gets nothing.
3. **SeniorLiving.org, "Best Medical Alert Systems of 2026: Tested and Ranked"** — **ECM competitor. Scanned, not cited, never linked.** Angle: hands-on testing, ranked. Gap: same as above.
4. **Retirement Living, "6 Best Medical Alert Systems of 2026"** — retirementliving.com. Angle: tested and reviewed ranked list. Gap: no independent evidence on fall detection accuracy.
5. **Consumer Reports** — the one outlier. Its "how to handle accidental activations" piece is the only mainstream article treating false alarms and response protocol seriously. **Gap:** paywalled for most readers, and its buying content is still brand-first.

Broad on device types and a good question list. **Gaps:** no cost figures at all, no Medicare or Medicaid section, no scam section, no evidence on whether fall detection works.

**Structural pattern across the SERP:** ranked brand lists, refreshed annually, monetised by referral. Every one of them has a commercial reason not to say "fall detection misses a lot of falls" or "the cheapest useful answer may be an unmonitored button."

## The gap

> Everybody ranks the brands. Nobody tells the family the three things that actually determine whether this purchase saves their parent's life: that a device only works if it is worn and pressed, and the research on the very old shows call alarms often were not used in exactly the falls that ended in a long lie; that automatic fall detection tested on real falls performs far below its marketing; and that the money lives in activation fees, leased equipment, auto-renewal, and the fall-detection add-on rather than the advertised monthly price. ECM takes no referral fee, so it is the only site on the results page that can print all three.

A second, smaller gap nobody has taken: **a state Medicaid PERS regulation doubles as a free minimum specification sheet.** Virginia's rule requires waterproof wearable activator, low-battery signalling, hands-free two-way voice, line seizure, FCC approval, UL 1635 and UL 1637, operators 24 hours a day every day of the year, and equipment repaired or replaced within 24 hours. A family buying privately can demand exactly that list. No competitor has framed it this way.

## YMYL landmines (blocking, for creator and publisher)

1. **Original Medicare does not pay for medical alert systems.** Say it plainly. Do not soften it to "may not cover." Do not link medicare.gov (403).
2. **Never say Medicare Advantage covers it.** Say some plans may offer it as a supplemental benefit and the only way to know is the plan's Evidence of Coverage or the number on the card.
3. **Never state a Medicaid asset limit, income cap, or look-back period.** Not one number. PERS is described as a waiver service that varies by state; eligibility goes to an elder law attorney or the state Medicaid agency.
4. **Every dollar figure must carry "NCOA" and a date in the body text.** All cost figures in this file trace to NCOA's cost guide of 5 January 2026 or its February 2025 survey. No other prices.
5. **Never publish a specific brand's price.** None are in this file.
6. **No system detects every fall.** This must appear explicitly. Sourced to Bagalà et al. 2012 in PLOS ONE.
7. **No medical advice.** Do not tell readers a device is indicated for cardiac, seizure, or dementia conditions. Describe the situations that make a device more useful and send clinical questions to the parent's doctor.
8. **Do not promise a response time.** No certification guarantees how fast help reaches a specific home.
9. **Cite the BMJ long-lie study with its limitation.** UK, 110 participants, all over 90. Not a US national statistic.
10. **Fear as a call to action is banned** by brand.md. The falls data is context for a decision, never a scare.
11. **Do not restate the 94% to 25% fall detection figure.** Untraceable. Excluded.
12. **The Consumer Reports 62 percent figure has no confirmed survey year.** Either date it or drop the number and keep the qualitative point.
13. The word "the elderly" appears inside the official name "Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly." **Do not name PACE.** If PACE is relevant, define it functionally: a combined Medicare and Medicaid programme for older adults who qualify for nursing home level care but want to stay at home.
