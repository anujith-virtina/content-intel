Introduction

The keys are hanging by the door, and nobody has touched them in a week. The driveway is empty, and Thursday is the day your parent used to drive to the pharmacy alone.

Deciding your parent should stop driving is only half the problem. The other half, which almost nothing online addresses, is comparing senior transportation options and building a plan that replaces the car.

If your family is still weighing whether it's time, taking away the keys is covered elsewhere. This post is for the family that has already made that call.

Here you'll find which service fits which trip, how far ahead to book each one, and what to do the day a ride falls through.

What Trips Should Your Transportation Plan Cover First?

Start by listing the trips that actually happen every week, not the ones that come to mind first.

  • Medical appointments, the ones on the calendar and the ones added last minute
  • Groceries and the pharmacy
  • Worship, if that matters to your parent
  • At least one purely social outing a week

Most of these trips are probably already covered by a family member. AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving report that 79% of family caregivers provide transportation, in Caregiving in the US 2025.

Start the plan by naming what family already provides, then fill only the real gaps.

Write the list down. Next to each trip, note who covers it now, and circle the ones nobody has an answer for. Those circled trips are the gaps this post exists to fill.

Which Senior Transportation Options Fit Which Trips?

No single service covers every trip. Match the service to the trip instead of picking one option for everything.

Recurring medical trips that can be booked ahead usually fit a scheduled service. Same-day errands fit a rideshare or taxi. Trips where your parent benefits from company fit a volunteer driver.

ADA paratransit is a shared-ride service transit agencies must run near their fixed bus routes, under federal rule 49 CFR Part 37. It's booked at least one day ahead, through a disability-based certification, not an age cutoff.

NEMT, non-emergency medical transportation, is a separate Medicaid benefit, formalized in the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2021. It exists for enrollees with no other way to reach a covered medical appointment.

Rideshare and concierge-style services built on it are booked same-day at the standard fare, with no subsidy. Volunteer driver programs and community-based ride networks run in specific areas and typically want a few days' notice to match a driver to the trip.

Veterans have two doors. VA transportation books rides at least 48 hours ahead and allows one companion. A separate DAV volunteer network is ambulatory only and not wheelchair accessible.

Some assisted living communities and memory care communities run their own scheduled transportation for residents. That's worth considering if transportation is one of several needs pushing your family toward a move.

Does Medicare Pay for Rides to the Doctor?

Original Medicare does not cover routine transportation to medical appointments. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer limited transportation as a supplemental benefit, but coverage varies by plan, by county, and by year, and is never guaranteed.

Families should check a specific plan's Evidence of Coverage before counting on this benefit. Medicare and CMS do not guarantee it, and it can change from one plan year to the next.

Non-emergency medical transportation, or NEMT, works differently, because it's a Medicaid benefit, not a Medicare one. It exists for Medicaid enrollees who have no other way to reach a covered appointment. Eligibility rules vary by state, so questions about your parent's specific situation belong with an elder law attorney.

One option combines Medicare and Medicaid into a single program. It serves older adults who qualify for nursing-home-level care but want to stay home. Transportation to the program's day center and to medical appointments is included at no separate cost, according to CMS.

Ask the program directly whether it serves your parent's area.

How Far Ahead Do You Need to Book Each Ride?

Booking windows range from immediate to about two days. Mismatching the window to the trip is the most common planning mistake.

Rideshare and taxi rides are immediate. ADA paratransit needs at least one day ahead, under 49 CFR 37.131(b). VA transportation needs at least 48 hours ahead.

Volunteer and community ride networks typically want a few days' notice, because they're matching a specific driver to your parent's trip. NEMT and Medicare Advantage transportation benefits vary by state, by broker, or by plan, so call ahead and ask directly.

A plan built only around a same-day service or only around a scheduled one eventually fails. A workable plan uses at least one of each.

How Do You Plan a Low-Stakes First Ride?

The first ride with any new service should be short, familiar, and scheduled well before it's actually needed. Never make the first ride a trip to a hospital appointment.

Pick a destination your parent already knows, like a favorite lunch spot, a sibling's house, or a regular hair appointment. Schedule it on a calm day, never the day of a medical appointment. A late pickup then becomes a minor inconvenience, not a missed test.

Ride along for the first trip if the service allows a companion, so your parent has company while learning how pickup and drop-off work. Afterward, ask what felt awkward. Common surprises include a shared-ride window that runs long, or a driver who stays in the car.

This first ride is a test, not a verdict. It doesn't guarantee a smooth service every time after.

What Happens When a Ride Falls Through?

Every plan needs a named backup, because shared-ride scheduling and volunteer availability both fail sometimes. The day it happens is the wrong day to start searching.

Identify a backup for each recurring trip: a family member on call, a second volunteer program, or a standby rideshare account.

Riders using shared-ride paratransit commonly cite unpredictable pickup windows as their main frustration, according to caregiver transportation guidance from AARP and the Family Caregiver Alliance. That's exactly why a backup matters most for medical trips.

For families who can't build and monitor this system themselves, care management professionals can set up and track the whole schedule, including backups. Keep every phone number, login, and booking step in one place. Then any family member can step in.

A backup doesn't need to be free. One that costs a little and works beats a free one nobody can reach in time.

What Does Losing a Car Really Cost Your Parent?

The cost that matters most isn't the car payment. It's the risk of your parent seeing fewer people and doing less.

Roughly a quarter of community-dwelling adults 65 and older are socially isolated, according to the National Institute on Aging. NIA's research on isolation and health risks links loneliness to higher rates of heart disease, depression, cognitive decline, and earlier death.

This is a reason to build the plan promptly, not a warning that a parent without a car is doomed to isolation. The warning signs of social isolation and loneliness are worth knowing regardless of transportation.

When the car goes, so do its ongoing costs: insurance, fuel, and maintenance, worth factoring into what your family budgets for rides.

Transportation gaps are a well-documented reason older adults miss or delay medical care, which is exactly why the plan is worth building now.

What Questions Should You Ask Before You Book a Ride Service?

Ask these on the phone before the first ride, and write down the answers.

  1. What is your service area, and does it cover both my parent's home and the appointment address?
  2. How far ahead do I need to book, and is that different for a first-time rider?
  3. Is this a shared ride, and how many stops might be added along the way?
  4. Do you offer door-to-door service, or only curb-to-curb?
  5. Can a family member or companion ride along at no extra step?
  6. What happens if the driver is late or the trip is canceled at the last minute?
  7. Is there a cost, and how and when is it billed?
  8. What is your cancellation policy if my parent needs to reschedule?

Check This Before Your Parent's First Ride

None of this takes long, and doing it now avoids a scramble later.

  • The week's recurring trips mapped and written down
  • One scheduled service identified for medical appointments
  • One same-day service identified for spontaneous trips
  • Booking lead time confirmed for each service chosen
  • A low-stakes first ride scheduled before any trip that matters
  • A named backup option in place for at least one trip
  • Every phone number and login saved where any family member can find it

People Also Ask

Can My Parent Use a Rideshare App Without a Smartphone?

Yes. Concierge-style services book a rideshare by phone instead of an app. A family member can also book and pay for a ride remotely. Ask any service directly whether phone-based booking is available before assuming an app is required.

Is Public Transportation Free for Older Adults?

It depends on the local transit agency. Many systems offer a reduced senior fare, and some waive fares during off-peak hours, but there's no single national program that makes public transit free. Check with the transit agency serving your parent's address.

Does Medicaid Pay for a Ride to Every Doctor's Appointment?

Not automatically. NEMT covers rides to Medicaid-covered appointments. The state or a managed care plan arranges them, so you do not book directly. Eligibility and coverage questions belong with the state Medicaid office or an elder law attorney.

What if My Parent Lives Somewhere With No Paratransit Service?

ADA paratransit only exists near fixed bus routes, so many rural areas have none. Volunteer driver networks and region-specific rural transit programs often fill that gap, and the Eldercare Locator can identify what actually serves that ZIP code.

Conclusion

Senior transportation options work best combined, not chosen once. Your parent can get around safely without driving, if the family builds a plan instead of improvising. One service for scheduled medical trips, one for same-day errands, and a backup for the day something falls through, that's the whole shape of it.

The trips don't stop because the driving does, so the plan is worth building this week.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is NADTC Still a Place to Call for Transportation Help?

No. NADTC's federal technical assistance agreement ended September 30, 2025, and no successor center has been named. Its website remains online as a resource library, but it no longer offers live, one-on-one help. Call the Eldercare Locator instead.

How Do I Find Out What Serves My Parent's Specific Address?

Call the Eldercare Locator at 800-677-1116, weekdays 8am to 9pm Eastern. It connects callers to the Area Agency on Aging covering that address, part of a network of more than 600 across the country.

Can a Family Member Get Paid or Reimbursed for Driving?

Sometimes. Some Medicaid and VA programs reimburse mileage to a family member who drives. The rules vary by state and program. Ask the state Medicaid office or the VA location handling your parent's care.

What Is the Program That Combines Medicare and Medicaid for Transportation?

One option combines both programs for older adults who qualify for nursing-home-level care but want to stay home. It arranges transportation to its day center and to medical appointments at no separate cost, confirmed directly with the program.

How Do I Know if a Volunteer Driver Network Serves My Parent's Town?

These programs operate only in specific communities, so coverage is not guaranteed. The Eldercare Locator can identify whether one serves your parent's address, along with any local Area Agency on Aging programs.

What's a Simple Backup for the Occasional Canceled Ride?

Keep a rideshare or taxi account set up and ready, even if it isn't your main plan. Save the login where any family member can access it, so a canceled ride does not turn into a missed appointment.

Can a Companion Ride Along on Paratransit or a VA Ride?

VA transportation allows one companion when the appointment is authorized. Paratransit companion policies vary by transit agency, so ask when you book.
