Introduction

You are probably comparing these two options because something changed. A fall, a hospital discharge, a bad night, or a slow sense that the current arrangement has stopped working.

Adult day care and a nursing home are not two points on the same scale. One is a service your parent attends and comes home from. The other is where your parent lives.

This guide compares the hours, the 2025 median costs, who pays, and the signs that a day program has run out of room.

The Real Difference Is Who Is Responsible Overnight

An adult day center is open roughly seven to 10 hours a day, according to the Alzheimer's Association. That leaves around 14 hours in every 24 for somebody else to cover.

In most families, that somebody is you. So the useful question is not how sick your parent is. It is who is responsible between 6pm and 8am.

What Adult Day Care Actually Covers

A day center provides supervision, meals, activities, help with personal care, and in some programs health monitoring. Many run their own transport, and many do not.

Programs come in three models, and only the health-focused model includes skilled nursing on the floor. Our adult day care guide covers the models and what to ask before enrolling.

What a Nursing Home Actually Covers

The National Institute on Aging describes nursing homes as providing nursing care, 24-hour supervision, three meals a day, and rehabilitation therapies. They also help with everyday activities, for people who cannot be cared for at home.

Staffing is regulated, and the standing federal baseline is licensed nursing coverage 24 hours a day. KFF described it in April 2025 as including a registered nurse on duty at least eight consecutive hours a day, seven days a week.

Nursing homes are licensed by the state, and federally certified when they take Medicare or Medicaid.

How the Two Costs Compare

These are not the same kind of bill. Adult day health care is priced per day attended, so the bill scales with the days your parent goes.

A nursing home is priced per resident day, with room and board included. The only way to change that bill is to leave.

The national median for adult day health care is $95 a day. At five days a week, that is about $24,700 a year. Both figures come from the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey.

The same survey puts the median for a semi-private nursing home room at $315 a day, or $114,975 a year. A private room runs to $355 a day, or $129,575 a year.

These are medians, so half of providers charge more, and your local rate may differ.

Assisted living sits between the two, at a median of $6,200 a month. Our breakdown of what assisted living costs sets out what that rate includes.

Whether any of this is tax deductible is a question for a tax professional.

Who Pays for Each Option

The two options are funded from almost entirely different places.

Where Medicare Stops

Medicare does not pay for long-term custodial care in a nursing home. It covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing facility care per benefit period, after a qualifying hospital stay. A doctor has to certify that daily skilled care is needed.

That coverage is short-term and rehabilitative. Once it ends, the cost falls to your family, to Medicaid if your parent qualifies, or to long-term care insurance.

Custodial care means help with everyday tasks such as bathing, dressing, and eating. That is most of what a nursing home provides day to day.

Medicare does not cover adult day care either, for the same reason.

Where Medicaid Comes In

Medicaid is the primary payer for most nursing home residents. KFF reported that more than 60% of the 1.2 million people in nursing homes as of July 2024 had Medicaid as their primary payer.

Medicaid may also cover adult day services in some states, through home and community based services waivers, usually shortened to HCBS. Coverage varies by state.

Eligibility rules vary by state and change regularly. Speak to an elder law attorney in your state before you move any money or file anything.

Signs Adult Day Care Is No Longer Enough

The Family Caregiver Alliance names this pivot directly. It describes the point at which a person can no longer attend adult day care, leaving the family covering every hour.

Below are the signals it and the Alzheimer's Association list. None of them is a verdict on your parent, and none is a verdict on you.

  • Nights. Your parent is awake, unsettled, or unsafe at 3am. The Family Caregiver Alliance names sleeplessness, and no day program covers it.
  • Wandering. The Alzheimer's Association reports that six in 10 people living with dementia will wander at least once. Secured settings are what memory care communities are built for.
  • Incontinence care that has become constant rather than occasional.
  • Transfers that now take two people, or that are no longer safe for you alone.
  • Skilled nursing needs. The Family Caregiver Alliance names tube feeding, and wound and skin care.
  • Behavior changes linked to dementia that a center cannot safely manage on its floor.
  • A hospital stay or a crisis that resets what your parent can do alone.
  • Your own health. The Family Caregiver Alliance treats a caregiver's declining physical health as a real factor.

The Alzheimer's Association places the need for 24-hour supervision in the middle stages of Alzheimer's. A center open seven to 10 hours a day cannot meet a 24-hour need, however good it is.

Take any of these to your parent's doctor or a care manager before you act on them.

Your own capacity belongs in this decision. AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving found that over half of family caregivers handle complex medical or nursing tasks. Only 11% were trained for them.

The same report, Caregiving in the US 2025, released in July 2025, found one in five caregivers in fair or poor health. That is a reason to bring in help.

Is There an Option Between the Two?

Yes, and most families land in one of three places rather than at either extreme.

The first is adult day care plus paid help at home for evenings, nights, or weekends. Home care providers cover the hours a center cannot. Our comparison of in-home care vs nursing homes weighs that route in detail.

The second is assisted living, which sits between the two. Assisted living communities give your parent daily help in a residential setting, but not nursing care.

The third is PACE. It is a combined Medicare and Medicaid program for older adults who qualify for nursing home level care but want to stay home.

PACE serves people aged 55 and over in a PACE service area, delivered mainly through an adult day health center. Availability varies by state.

Questions to Ask Before You Decide

Take these to a nursing home tour, a discharge planner, or a care manager call. Written answers let you compare two options honestly.

  1. What level of care has my parent been assessed at, and who did the assessment?
  2. What is the all-in daily rate, and what is billed separately?
  3. Do you accept Medicaid, and under what conditions?
  4. What licensed nursing coverage is on site overnight and at weekends?
  5. What is the staff-to-resident ratio on the evening and night shifts?
  6. How do you handle a resident who wanders, and is the unit secured?
  7. If this begins as a rehabilitation stay, what decides whether it becomes permanent?
  8. What would have to change for my parent to be able to come home again?

Question eight is the one families forget to ask. The answer tells you whether you are arranging a stay or a move.

Use This Decision Checklist

Have all of this in hand before the family conversation. It shortens the argument considerably.

  • A written list of the hours your parent is unsafe alone, split into days and nights
  • Your parent's assessed care level in writing, from their doctor or a care manager
  • The daily rate for the day center your parent attends now, or the two nearest centers
  • Written all-in daily rates from two nursing homes you would actually use
  • Whether either option is covered by Medicaid in your state, confirmed by an elder law attorney
  • Whether a PACE program operates in your parent's area
  • A named person who covers nights and weekends if you stay with the day program
  • An honest note on your own health, work, and limits, before the family meeting

Bring the written version to the call with your siblings.

People Also Ask

Is Adult Day Care Cheaper Than a Nursing Home?

Yes, substantially. The 2025 medians are $95 a day for adult day health care and $315 a day for a semi-private nursing home room, per CareScout. That rate includes room, board, and nursing around the clock.

Does Medicare Pay for a Nursing Home?

Not for long-term care. Medicare covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing care per benefit period, after a qualifying hospital stay. After that the bill falls to your family, to Medicaid if your parent qualifies, or to insurance.

Can Adult Day Care Keep My Parent Out of a Nursing Home?

It can extend how long staying at home works, but nobody should plan on it. Research on whether day programs postpone a move to residential care is mixed. A center open seven to 10 hours a day cannot cover a 24-hour need.

Is a Nursing Home the Same as Assisted Living?

No. Assisted living provides help with daily activities in a residential community, with staff on site. A nursing home provides ongoing nursing care for people who can no longer be cared for at home.

Conclusion

The answer turns on one question: is your parent safe alone at night? If yes, adult day care is almost certainly where to start, and it costs a fraction of the alternative.

If the answer is no, no day program can fix that. The comparison is then between a nursing home and round-the-clock care at home.

If you are unsure, that is a level-of-care question rather than a budget one. Care management professionals assess exactly this and put it in writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can My Parent Go Back to a Day Center After a Nursing Home Stay?

Often yes, if the stay was rehabilitative and your parent comes home. The center will usually want a fresh assessment first, because needs change during a hospital stay.

What Is the Difference Between a Nursing Home and Memory Care?

Memory care is a secured residential setting built around dementia, with trained staff and adapted routines. A nursing home is built around ongoing nursing needs, which many residents with dementia also have.

What Is the Difference Between a Rehabilitation Stay and a Permanent Move?

A rehabilitation stay has a goal and an end date, usually returning home after an illness or surgery. A permanent move has neither. Ask at admission which one this is.

Can We Use Adult Day Care and Home Care Together?

Yes, and many families do. The center covers weekday hours, and a paid caregiver covers evenings, early mornings, or weekends. Price the combination before you rule it out.

What If There Is No Adult Day Center Near Us?

Availability varies a lot by area, and rural families often have no center within a reasonable drive. Your local Area Agency on Aging can tell you what operates nearby.

How Do We Decide When Siblings Disagree?

Start with facts rather than opinions: an assessed care level in writing, two written rates, and the hours nobody is covering. If disagreement holds, a care manager can chair the conversation.
