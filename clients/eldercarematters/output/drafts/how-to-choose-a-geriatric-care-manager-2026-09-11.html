Introduction

You have decided your parent needs more coordination than you can give. Now you are looking at a list of people who all describe themselves in almost the same words.

A geriatric care manager, also called an aging life care professional, assesses your parent's situation and coordinates their care. The role itself, and what a care manager does, is covered separately.

The hard part is not finding one. It is telling one from another, because the title itself is not a license.

This post covers which credentials mean something, how to confirm them, what the hours cost, and who is paying your care manager.

Is Geriatric Care Manager a Licensed Title?

No. There is no national license for geriatric care managers, and no governmental body regulates the title.

The Tax Adviser, the journal of the American Institute of CPAs, reported this in June 2019. Care managers are not regulated by any governmental entity, and anyone may use the title.

That is only half the picture. The National Institute on Aging describes a geriatric care manager as usually a licensed nurse or social worker.

Those underlying state licenses are regulated. A nursing or clinical social work license carries a disciplinary record, and a state board can suspend or revoke it.

So both things are true at once. The title is not regulated, and the person underneath the title usually is.

That combination is your actual situation, and it has one practical consequence. Because the title tells you nothing, you have to ask what license and what certification sit underneath it.

This is not a warning about the people doing this work. Most care managers are careful professionals, and many hold state licenses that took years to earn.

Which Care Manager Credentials Actually Mean Something?

Three certifications and two professional licenses matter here, and they certify different things.

A state license is stronger protection, because a board can revoke it and a certification body largely cannot.

Credential Issued By What It Requires Renewal What It Does Not Tell You
CMC, Care Manager Certified National Academy of Certified Care Managers Education plus paid, supervised care management work within ten years Every three years, with continuing education Anything about fees, ownership, or referral payments
CCM, Certified Case Manager Commission for Case Manager Certification Health or human services license or degree, plus case management experience Every five years, 80 continuing education hours, 8 in ethics Whether the holder has worked with older adults
C-SWCM and C-ASWCM, social work case manager credentials National Association of Social Workers Social work degree, bachelor's level, or master's for the advanced Confirm current terms with the issuing body That it is a license, because it is not
RN, registered nurse Your parent's state nursing board State nursing education and examination Varies by state board Any specific training in care management
LCSW, licensed clinical social worker Your parent's state social work board Master's in social work plus supervised clinical hours Varies by state board Any training in care management

CCM is a general case management credential, not an aging-specific one. Many holders are discharge planners or insurance case managers.

A CCM may have spent a career in workers' compensation and never worked with older adults. Ask what their case management experience was in.

The CMC filter is tighter, because the National Academy of Certified Care Managers requires paid, supervised work within ten years. It says a job title alone does not qualify anyone.

What Does Aging Life Care Association Membership Actually Tell You?

Less than you might think. Membership comes in levels, and only the top level requires a certification at all.

The Aging Life Care Association, usually shortened to ALCA, sets three membership levels.

  • Advanced Professional: a degree, supervised aging life care experience, and one of the certifications above
  • Professional: a degree and experience, with no certification required
  • Associate: a degree, a nursing diploma, or a practical nurse, physical therapist assistant, or occupational therapist assistant license, plus a paid role

A family told only that someone is an ALCA member has learned almost nothing. Ask at what level, and which certification qualified them.

The association formed in 1985 and reported nearly 2,000 members nationwide on 29 April 2026.

Two thousand members nationwide means many capable care managers are not members, and many areas have none nearby. Membership is a useful signal and a complaint route, not a license and not a requirement.

How Do You Check That a Credential Is Real?

Every claim above is checkable by you, free, in about five minutes. Check on the issuing body's own site, not the care manager's.

  1. A nursing license. Nursys, the national verification service from the National Council of State Boards of Nursing, is free to the public. It returns license status and publicly available discipline for participating boards.
  2. A clinical social work license. There is no national database, so use the state board's own lookup, which the Association of Social Work Boards links to. Expect status, type, expiry, and disciplinary actions.
  3. A CMC. The National Academy of Certified Care Managers takes verification requests through a form on its site.
  4. A CCM. The Commission for Case Manager Certification runs a public verification lookup.
  5. ALCA membership. Use the association's member search and read the level, which is the part that matters. Its public number is 520-881-8008.

Once the credentials check out, search for care management professionals serving your parent's area.

Who Is Actually Paying Your Care Manager?

Most care managers are paid only by the family. Some also own, or are paid by, businesses they might recommend. Ask, and get the answer in writing.

The Aging Life Care Association's Code of Ethics and Standards of Practice, revised June 2025, covers this. Members must disclose business, professional, or personal relationships when they make a referral.

Any separate business that takes or offers referral fees must run independently from the practice. It also advises against a care manager paying themselves from client funds, and suggests outside oversight.

The association reviews complaints against members through its Professional Conduct Review Committee and a public form. A care manager who belongs to no association has no such route.

The wider issue sits with referral agencies, some of which collect a fee on a move-in.

Georgia Senate Bill 439, effective 1 July 2026, requires referral agencies serving assisted living communities to disclose how they are paid. Families must also be told other options may exist.

Washington required the same disclosure in 2011, plus $1 million in liability insurance.

These laws regulate referral agencies, not care managers. A care manager paid only by your family collects no referral fee. One who also runs a referral business does.

So ask whether they receive any payment from the home care providers they recommend. Get that answer in the engagement agreement.

What Does a Geriatric Care Manager Cost?

Expect to pay by the hour, and expect the first assessment to be the biggest bill.

AARP guidance on care managers, updated 5 August 2026, puts the rate at $150 to $300 an hour depending on region.

AARP, in the same 5 August 2026 guidance, says an initial assessment typically runs 4 to 6 hours of billable time. Some care managers also bill mileage and travel time. Get the fee structure in writing first.

The Tax Adviser, June 2019, adds a sharp question. Ask what billing increment is used, 6, 10, or 15 minutes, because it changes the bill more than the rate.

The National Institute on Aging says Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for geriatric care management. Most private health insurance does not either, so most families pay out of pocket.

Long-term care insurance may cover part of it, the institute notes. AARP adds that some employee assistance programs cover part of the fee. Medigap and Medicare Advantage plans do not.

Call your benefits line and ask whether your employee assistance program covers care management.

If Medicaid planning comes up, that is a legal and financial question that varies by state. Take it to an elder law attorney.

Questions to Ask Before You Hire a Care Manager

Ask these on the first call, write the answers down, and notice any hesitation.

  1. What license do you hold, in which state, and what is the number?
  2. Which care management certification do you hold, and when does it renew?
  3. If you belong to ALCA, at what level, and which certification qualified you?
  4. How many active clients are you carrying right now?
  5. Who covers your clients when you are away, ill, or unreachable?
  6. What is your after-hours availability, and what counts as an emergency?
  7. What is your rate, your billing increment, and do you bill mileage and calls?
  8. How many hours is the assessment, what do we receive, and when?
  9. Do you or your company hold a financial interest in any home care agency, community, or referral agency?
  10. Do you ever receive a referral fee or commission from a provider you recommend?
  11. Will you put that answer in writing in the engagement agreement?
  12. If we do not take your recommendation, does anything change about your fee?

Items four through six are the ones almost nobody asks, and item eleven makes a verbal answer enforceable.

What Are the Warning Signs You Should Walk Away?

Hesitation on any question about money, coverage, or independence. Each is a reason to keep looking, not proof of anything.

  • Will not give a license, a state, and a license number
  • Cannot name which certification they hold, or names one nobody issues
  • Refuses to put fees in writing, or omits mileage and travel
  • Cannot say who covers the account when they are away
  • Is paid by, or part-owns, a provider they recommend, and did not say so
  • Recommends a specific community on the first call, before any assessment
  • Promises a specific outcome, a wait list bypass, or a Medicaid result
  • Asks to be paid from accounts they manage for your parent

Do You Actually Need a Paid Care Manager?

Not always. Paid care management earns its fee where distance, complexity, family conflict, or your own time is the constraint.

The National Institute on Aging describes care managers as especially helpful when a caregiver lives far away. AARP frames the value partly around a working caregiver's time.

That threshold, and the point at which shared caregiving stops working, often arrives alongside caregiver burnout.

If you live nearby, needs are stable, the family agrees, and you have the hours, you may not need to pay.

Your Area Agency on Aging offers options counseling and care coordination, the Administration for Community Living says. The Eldercare Locator connects you to the one serving your parent's address.

Services vary by area, many have waiting lists, and some are targeted by income.

A care manager can be bought in hours rather than by the year, so a one-time assessment is a fair stopping point.

Check These Before You Sign the Agreement

All of it is easier before money changes hands.

  • A written fee agreement naming rate, increment, and travel
  • The license number confirmed on the state board's own lookup
  • The certification confirmed with the issuing body
  • The association membership level confirmed, not just the membership
  • A written answer on referral fees and any ownership interest
  • Two references from families served longer than six months
  • Written agreement on what the assessment delivers, and when
  • A named backup for vacations, illness, and after-hours calls

People Also Ask

What Is the Difference Between a Geriatric Care Manager and an Aging Life Care Professional?

They describe the same work. Aging life care professional is the association's term, and geriatric care manager is the older name.

Who Does a Geriatric Care Manager Work For, Your Parent or You?

Usually whoever signs the agreement and pays. Ask who is named as the client, and who receives the reports.

Can a Care Manager Help If You Live in Another State?

Yes, and the National Institute on Aging calls this one of its most useful situations. Press hardest on the coverage questions, because you cannot drop in.

Conclusion

The right care manager is the one whose license you confirmed on a state board, and whose certification you confirmed with the issuer.

It is also the one whose fee agreement is in writing, and who told you in writing that nobody else is paying them.

The title is not regulated, so the checking is yours to do, and it takes an evening.

Start with a care management directory for your parent's area, then work down the twelve questions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can You Hire a Care Manager for Just One Assessment?

Usually yes. A one-time assessment with a written plan is a bounded engagement, and ongoing management is optional.

Does Long-Term Care Insurance Pay for Care Management?

It may cover part of it, according to the National Institute on Aging. It depends on the policy, so ask the insurer in writing.

Could Your Employer's Benefits Cover Part of the Fee?

Sometimes. AARP, in guidance updated 5 August 2026, notes that some employee assistance programs cover part of care management fees.

What Should You Gather Before the First Meeting?

A current medication list, insurance cards, recent discharge paperwork, and legal documents such as a power of attorney. Gathering them first keeps billable hours off paperwork.

What Can You Do If the Working Relationship Goes Wrong?

End it on the notice terms in your agreement. A state nursing or social work board takes complaints about that license. ALCA reviews complaints about its members.

Can a Care Manager Make Decisions for Your Parent?

No. Hiring one gives them no legal authority. That comes from a power of attorney or health care proxy, prepared with an elder law attorney.
