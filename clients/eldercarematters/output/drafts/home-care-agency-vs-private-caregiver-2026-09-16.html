Introduction

Your parent needs more help at home than your family can keep providing alone, and you are staring down two very different paths. The home care agency vs private caregiver decision looks like a question about hourly rates. It is really a question about which jobs your family can take on.

An agency's price covers screening, payroll, supervision, and a substitute when your regular caregiver cannot come. A privately hired caregiver usually costs less per hour, but your family absorbs everything the agency would otherwise handle. Neither route is automatically the smarter choice, and the right answer depends on your family's time, budget, and comfort with paperwork.

This guide walks through what each option actually includes and what you take on legally if you hire someone directly. It also covers what Medicare and Medicaid will and will not pay for either way. By the end, you will have a clear checklist and a list of questions to ask before you hire anyone.

Home Care Agency vs Private Caregiver: What Each Option Actually Includes

A home care agency employs, trains, and schedules caregivers, and sends someone to your parent's home under a service contract. A privately hired caregiver works directly for your family, with no company standing between you.

Some families value the managed process an agency provides. Others prefer the lower rate and directness of hiring someone themselves.

You can compare local home care providers by category once you know what each route actually asks of your family. The table below breaks down the real differences, not just the sticker price.

Who Covers a Sick Day or Vacation The agency supplies a substitute The family arranges its own backup coverage

What You Take On When You Hire a Caregiver Directly

Hiring a caregiver directly generally makes your family a household employer, not a customer. That status comes with real paperwork: an employer identification number, payroll tax withholding, work-eligibility verification, and annual reporting. In many states it also means carrying workers' compensation insurance, though the requirement varies by state.

None of these numbers or thresholds are stable enough to print here, since they change every year and differ by state. Talk to a tax professional before you take on this role.

Loop in an elder law attorney if your parent's care involves any Medicaid planning. A care manager can also supervise the day-to-day details of a private hire if your family would rather not do it alone.

None of this makes hiring directly a bad idea. It simply means the paperwork lands on your family instead of an agency's back office.

AARP's Hiring an In-Home Caregiver guide walks through this responsibility in more detail.

The 1099 Mistake Families Make

A caregiver who works in your home, on your schedule, using your supplies, is rarely a legitimate independent contractor. Paying by 1099 to skip the paperwork is the mistake, not a shortcut. A tax professional can tell you how your specific arrangement should be classified.

Wage and Overtime Rules Are Still Changing

Since 2013, federal minimum wage and overtime rules have applied to home care workers. Agencies could not claim an older exemption for this type of work.

In July 2025, the Department of Labor proposed reversing that rule for agencies and paused enforcement while the proposal moves forward. This is genuinely unsettled, so confirm the current status with a tax professional or employment attorney before you assume either version applies to your situation.

This distinction matters most if you are comparing an agency's guaranteed overtime pay against a private arrangement that has not addressed overtime at all.

Who Covers You When the Caregiver Gets Sick or Quits

An agency supplies a substitute when your regular caregiver is sick, on vacation, or quits. A private hire means your family arranges its own backup, whether that is a second caregiver, a relative, or a respite option.

This is the least visible cost of hiring privately, and it rarely shows up until the day you actually need a backup. Build a coverage plan before you need it, not after.

A second caregiver on call, a rotating family schedule, or a short-term respite stay can each work, depending on your parent's needs.

When your parent's needs outpace what either route can supply at home, the next conversation is often assisted living. That is not a failure. It is simply a sign the level of care needs to change.

Who Handles Screening and Background Checks

Agencies run and stand behind their own background checks and credential verification as part of the service. When you hire privately, your family runs the background check, verifies references directly, and confirms work eligibility yourself.

Confirming a private caregiver's legal eligibility to work is your responsibility too, not a box an agency checks for you.

The Family Caregiver Alliance puts it plainly: always check references, always run a background check. If your parent's caregiver holds a credential such as an HHA or CNA certification, verify it directly with the issuing body. Do not rely on the caregiver's paperwork alone.

Comparing the Real Cost of Each Route

Compare structure, not just the hourly number. The CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey put the national median hourly rate for non-medical in-home care at $35 an hour.

That is up 3% from the year before. At 44 hours a week, that works out to roughly $80,080 a year.

The 2024 Genworth/CareScout survey reported similar annual medians: $77,792 for home health aide services and $75,504 for homemaker services, both based on a 44-hour week. Both figures are medians, not averages, and both come from the same annual survey series. These figures come from CareScout and Genworth's own survey series, not from either side of the industry that has a stake in your decision.

An agency's rate is bundled: recruiting, supervision, substitute coverage, insurance, and payroll are all built in. A privately negotiated wage looks lower, but your family separately absorbs payroll tax, insurance, and the time it takes to manage all of it. Ways to pay for in-home care are worth checking regardless of which route you choose.

What Medicare and Medicaid Actually Pay For

Medicare's home health benefit is narrow. It pays only when a doctor certifies that your parent needs skilled nursing or therapy care on a part-time or intermittent basis.

Your parent must also be considered homebound. Medicare does not cover ongoing, non-medical help with bathing, dressing, meals, or companionship, whether that help comes from an agency or a private hire.

Some state Medicaid programs offer self-directed home and community based services, which can pay a caregiver of your parent's choosing, sometimes including a family member. This is never automatic and works differently in every state. Questions about what your parent's state actually allows belong with a state Medicaid office and an elder law attorney.

Ask specifically whether your state's program would pay the caregiver you already have in mind, since not every state allows that.

Questions to Ask Before You Hire

These questions work whether your next call is to an agency or to a caregiver candidate. Bring this list and write down every answer.

  1. Who performs the background check and reference verification, and can I see how it was done?
  2. Who provides a substitute if my regular caregiver is sick, on vacation, or quits?
  3. Who is legally responsible for payroll taxes, workers' compensation, and liability if someone is hurt on the job?
  4. What certifications or training does this caregiver hold, and how is it verified?
  5. Is the current rate guaranteed in writing, and how are increases handled?
  6. If my parent's needs increase, can this same arrangement still meet them?
  7. Who do I call, day or night, if something goes wrong?
  8. If I hire privately, do I have a tax professional or payroll service lined up to handle withholding and insurance?

A longer list of questions to ask agencies covers what to ask on a tour or an intake call specifically.

A Decision Checklist Before You Choose

Use this checklist before you sign anything, whether you are hiring an agency or a caregiver directly.

  • I know whether my family can absorb payroll, tax, and insurance paperwork, or would rather pay an agency to handle it.
  • I have a real plan for coverage on a day the caregiver cannot work.
  • I have talked to a tax professional about household employer status if I am hiring directly.
  • I have talked to an elder law attorney about Medicaid or other legal questions specific to my parent.
  • I understand Medicare will not pay for this kind of ongoing daily help.
  • I have thought about whether my parent's care needs are likely to increase soon.
  • I have a way to verify a private caregiver's background and credentials myself.

Revisit this list any time your parent's needs change, since the right hire today may not fit in a year.

People Also Ask

Is It Cheaper to Hire a Caregiver Privately Than to Use an Agency?

Usually, yes, per hour. Once you add payroll tax, insurance, and backup coverage, the total cost gap narrows considerably. The honest comparison is structural, not just the hourly rate.

Can I Pay a Family Member to Provide Home Care?

Sometimes, through a specific state Medicaid self-direction program, but never automatically. Rules differ by state and by program. Ask your state Medicaid office what your parent's plan actually allows.

Do I Need a Background Check If I Hire a Caregiver Myself?

Yes. An agency runs this step for you. Hiring privately means your family arranges it directly, including reference checks and credential verification.

What Happens If My Privately Hired Caregiver Gets Hurt at Home?

Without workers' compensation coverage, your family can be financially exposed. Coverage requirements vary by state, so check what your state requires before you hire.

Conclusion

Neither a home care agency nor a privately hired caregiver is automatically the right answer. The home care agency vs private caregiver decision comes down to what your family can realistically manage. An agency's higher hourly rate covers paperwork and supervision for you, and a private hire leaves both jobs to your family.

If your family has the time, comfort with paperwork, and a real backup plan, hiring directly can work well and cost less overall. If your family would rather pay for someone else to handle screening, payroll, and coverage, an agency earns its rate.

Before you sign anything, talk to a tax professional about your obligations. Loop in an elder law attorney if Medicaid or any legal question touches your parent's situation. Both a care manager and an elder law attorney are searchable by category when you are ready for that conversation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Medicare Pay for a Home Caregiver?

No. Medicare's home health benefit covers only short-term, skilled, doctor-certified care for someone who is homebound. It does not cover ongoing help with bathing, dressing, meals, or companionship, whether that help is agency-provided or privately hired.

What Is a Household Employer, and Am I One?

If you hire and direct a caregiver in your home and supply the tools, you generally are a household employer. That status brings payroll and insurance obligations a tax professional can walk you through.

Can I Pay My Caregiver in Cash to Avoid the Paperwork?

Paying in cash does not remove your household-employer obligations. It just makes them easier to miss and more costly to fix later. A caregiver working under your direction, on your schedule, is rarely a legitimate 1099 contractor.

Does Medicaid Ever Pay a Family Caregiver?

In some states, yes, through a self-directed home and community based services program. It is state-specific and never automatic. Contact your state Medicaid office to ask what your parent qualifies for.

How Do I Check a Private Caregiver's References?

Call every reference directly and ask about reliability, punctuality, and how the caregiver handled a difficult day, not just whether they were competent. Verify any certification, such as HHA or CNA, with the issuing body itself, not the candidate's paperwork alone.

Is a Care Manager Worth It If I Hire Privately?

Many families use a care manager to supervise a privately hired caregiver, handle scheduling, and step in if something changes. It is a way of outsourcing the supervision without outsourcing the hire itself. A closer look at choosing a geriatric care manager covers what the role actually involves.
