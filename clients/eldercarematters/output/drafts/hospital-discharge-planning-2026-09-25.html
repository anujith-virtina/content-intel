Introduction

A doctor or case manager has just said the word discharge, and your family did not set the clock that started running. You may have 24 hours, or 72, but decisions that usually take weeks now have to happen fast.

This piece walks through what happens next, in the order it actually happens. You will learn what the hospital owes you, the one document and phone call that can slow things down, and where your parent can go.

A hospital discharge planning guide for families covers this ground in more depth, if a second reference alongside this one would help.

What Is the Hospital Required to Do Before My Parent Goes Home?

Federal rules require hospitals to build the discharge plan around your parent's own goals, not just an open bed. You and your parent count as partners in that process, not bystanders waiting to be told the plan.

The hospital must also hand you a list of Medicare-participating home health, rehab, and skilled nursing options serving your parent's area. That list has to include quality information about each one. You are free to choose among those options, and the hospital cannot steer you toward one.

Ask for that list by name if nobody has offered it yet. It is one of the few concrete things you can request today and expect to receive before your parent leaves.

Does It Matter If My Parent Was Admitted or Only Under Observation?

Yes, and it can decide whether Medicare later helps pay for skilled nursing care. That stay is sometimes called a SNF stay, meaning a covered stay in skilled nursing care. Original Medicare generally requires three consecutive midnights as a formally admitted inpatient before it will cover that stay.

Time spent under observation does not count toward those three midnights, no matter how many nights it runs.

This distinction gets a full explanation elsewhere. For the mechanics of admitted versus observation status, the Medicare observation trap covers it in detail, so this section will not repeat it.

Ask the nurse or case manager directly whether your parent is currently admitted or under observation. Ask for it in writing too, since hospitals typically document a change in status through a notice called the MOON, the Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice.

What Is the Important Message From Medicare, and Why Does It Matter Right Now?

This is the one document, and one phone call, that can actually slow discharge down. Medicare requires hospitals to give inpatients a written notice called the Important Message from Medicare, once at admission and again shortly before discharge.

The notice explains your right to ask for a fast review if your family disagrees with the discharge decision. Call the number printed on it to request a review from your area's Beneficiary and Family Centered Care Quality Improvement Organization, usually called the BFCC-QIO.

Filing that request generally keeps your parent in the hospital while the review happens. Ask the nurse or discharge planner for a copy of this notice today if your family has not already received one.

What Can I Do If I Think My Parent Is Being Sent Home Too Soon?

There is no single veto button here, but there are two real levers. First, tell the discharge planner in writing that your family disagrees, and ask what the hospital's grievance process is. Hospitals must have one and must tell you how to use it.

Second, if your parent is a Medicare inpatient, the BFCC-QIO fast appeal from the section above is the formal mechanism. Filing it pauses the discharge while reviewers look at the case, which buys your family real time.

Neither route guarantees your parent stays longer. Both routes guarantee someone with authority looks at the decision again before your parent walks out the door.

Where Can My Parent Go Next: Home, Rehab, or Skilled Nursing Care?

There are a small number of real options, and the right one depends on what your parent needs next, not what feels easiest today. The table below lays out what each setting is generally for, what Medicare covers, and what your family still has to arrange.

Setting Who It Usually Fits What Medicare Covers What Your Family Arranges
Home, No Added Services A parent who is stable and largely independent Nothing extra beyond ordinary follow-up care Everyday help, transportation, and safety changes at home
Home With Medicare Home Health A parent who needs short-term skilled nursing or therapy and is homebound Skilled nursing or therapy visits under a doctor's plan of care Everything outside that visit, including home care providers for meals, bathing, and supervision
A Rehab or Skilled Nursing Stay A parent who needs daily skilled care or therapy after a qualifying inpatient stay A cost-sharing schedule that changes by day range, covered in the next section Any stay beyond what Medicare covers, plus next steps after that stay
Assisted Living or Memory Care A parent who needs regular help with daily activities but not skilled nursing care Not covered by Medicare The full cost, unless a state Medicaid program or other source applies. An elder law attorney can explain the options.

None of these settings is the failure option. Many parents move between more than one of them as recovery progresses, and the first choice is rarely the only choice.

Will Medicare Pay for a Rehab or Skilled Nursing Stay?

Sometimes, and only under a specific structure. For a Medicare-covered stay in skilled nursing care following a qualifying hospital stay, the first 20 days generally carry no coinsurance at all.

Days 21 through 100 carry a daily coinsurance amount that changes every year, and after day 100 Medicare pays nothing. Confirm the current year's exact amounts directly with Medicare before your family counts on any number.

If your parent has a Medicare Advantage plan instead of Original Medicare, different rules can apply. Medicare Advantage plans set their own skilled nursing coverage rules, and the plan's own documents control. Call the plan directly rather than assuming Original Medicare's rules carry over.

Medicaid can sometimes pick up costs Medicare does not, but eligibility rules are complex and vary by state. Elder law attorneys handle exactly these questions and can explain what your parent's state actually allows.

What Will Medicare Home Health Actually Cover Once My Parent Is Home?

Less than most families expect, and this is the single most common misunderstanding about care after a hospital stay. Medicare home health requires your parent be homebound and need skilled nursing or therapy on an intermittent basis, ordered under a doctor's plan of care.

It does not cover 24-hour care, delivered meals, or help with shopping or cleaning. It also skips personal care if that is the only help your parent needs, per the National Council on Aging's home health overview. Families who assume home health means someone will be there are usually wrong.

Your family generally arranges everything else: safety changes at home, supervision, and daily help. Fall prevention and home modifications are worth reviewing before your parent walks back through the door.

You will likely be doing unpaid caregiving hours yourself in the weeks that follow, and most families eventually need a break. Who pays for respite care is worth reading before your family reaches that point.

What Should I Ask the Discharge Planner Before My Parent Leaves?

Bring these eight questions to your next conversation with the discharge planner, whether it happens by phone or in the room.

  1. Has the hospital given us the required list of Medicare-participating home health, rehab, and skilled nursing options serving our area?
  2. Was my parent formally admitted as an inpatient, or classified under observation, and for how many midnights?
  3. Have we received the Important Message from Medicare, and do we have the phone number for our area's BFCC-QIO?
  4. What specific skilled or therapy need would home health address, and who is the physician certifying the plan of care?
  5. Does this hospital stay meet Medicare's admission requirement for a covered rehab or skilled nursing stay, if my parent needs one?
  6. What has changed about my parent's ability to manage daily activities since before this hospital stay?
  7. Who is our point of contact if a problem comes up in the first week after discharge?
  8. What is the hospital's process if our family disagrees with the discharge plan?

A care management professional can help coordinate answers to these questions if your family cannot be there in person.

What Should I Check Off Before My Parent Walks Out the Door?

Use this checklist as a last pass, once every earlier answer is in hand.

  • We have the Important Message from Medicare and the BFCC-QIO phone number for our area
  • We know whether my parent was admitted as an inpatient or held under observation
  • We have the hospital's list of Medicare-participating home health, rehab, and skilled nursing options
  • We understand what Medicare home health will and will not cover
  • We have a plan for the first 24 hours home: medications, the follow-up appointment, and who will be present
  • We have contacted an elder law attorney if Medicaid or longer-term planning questions have come up
  • We have put any disagreement with the discharge plan in writing and given it to the discharge planner

Revisit any item still unchecked before your parent physically leaves the hospital.

People Also Ask

Can a Hospital Discharge My Parent Against My Family's Wishes?

Generally, yes, once the hospital's clinical and discharge planning process is complete. Your recourse is not a veto, it is the grievance process and, for a Medicare inpatient, the BFCC-QIO fast appeal described above.

How Long Can a Hospital Keep My Parent Under Observation Before Deciding on Admission?

There is no fixed limit written into that specific decision. Ask directly, in writing if needed, whether your parent has been formally admitted, since that status affects Medicare's later coverage rules.

Does a Hospital Have to Give Notice Before Discharging a Patient?

Yes. Hospitals must give written notice, including the Important Message from Medicare for inpatients, and must involve your family in planning where your parent goes next.

What Happens If My Parent Is Discharged and Something Goes Wrong at Home in the First Few Days?

Call your parent's physician or the hospital's discharge planning office first, since many hospitals track early problems after discharge. If the situation is urgent, treat it like any medical emergency and seek care right away.

Conclusion

A hospital discharge is not one decision. It is a sequence: what the hospital owes you, whether your parent was admitted or under observation, and the Important Message from Medicare. It also includes the setting that actually fits what your parent needs next.

You now know the one document and phone call that can slow things down if the timing feels wrong. You also know the questions to ask before anyone walks out the door.

If your parent's care needs continue past the first few weeks home, the next call depends on what has changed. It might be a home care provider, an assisted living community if needs have grown, or an elder law attorney for legal questions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Medicare Pay for Assisted Living After a Hospital Stay?

No. Medicare does not cover assisted living, memory care, or ongoing custodial care in any setting. It only covers specific, medically necessary care, such as short-term skilled nursing or home health.

What Is the MOON Notice, and Why Did My Parent Receive One?

MOON stands for Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice. Hospitals must give it to patients under observation rather than formally admitted, so your family knows in writing that inpatient rules do not yet apply.

Can I Ask for More Time Before My Parent Is Discharged?

You can ask, and the hospital may agree if your parent's medical condition supports it. If the answer is no, the BFCC-QIO fast appeal and the hospital's grievance process are your formal options.

Does Medicare Advantage Follow the Same Rules as Original Medicare for a Rehab Stay?

Not necessarily. Medicare Advantage plans set their own rules for a covered stay in skilled nursing care. Call the plan directly to ask what your parent's specific plan requires.

What if My Parent Was Already Living in an Assisted Living Community Before This Hospital Stay?

The discharge planning process works the same way, but your parent may return to that community instead of going home for the first time. Confirm directly with the community whether it can meet any new care needs your parent now has.

Who Decides Whether My Parent Needs a Rehab Stay Instead of Going Straight Home?

The hospital's medical team makes the clinical recommendation, based on what your parent needs to recover safely. Your family's job is to ask questions and weigh the options together.

Does Medicaid Ever Pay for a Nursing Home Stay After a Hospital Discharge?

In some circumstances, yes, but eligibility rules vary by state and change often. An elder law attorney can explain what your parent's state actually requires, since this varies too much for a blog post to answer.

Is There Ever a Right to Appeal Being Moved From Admitted to Observation Status?

A federal rule now lets some patients whose status was switched from inpatient to observation during the same stay appeal that change. Ask the hospital's patient advocate or call the number on your parent's MOON notice to find out if this applies to your situation.
