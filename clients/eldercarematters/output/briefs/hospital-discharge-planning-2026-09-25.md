# Brief: Hospital Discharge Planning — What to Do When a Parent Is Being Sent Home

## Thesis

A hospital discharge is not one decision, it is a sequence of six things that happen in order over 24 to 72 hours, and knowing that sequence, and the one document and one phone call that actually slow it down, is more useful to a frightened adult child than any list of definitions.

## Why this, why now, why us

- **Why this angle:** every competitor cluster (elder-law blogs, Medicare-shopping SEO pages, senior-living marketers) picks one lane, legal rights, or cost figures, or a setting to sell, and stops. Nobody walks the reader through the sequence they are actually living. Sequencing is the open gap and it matches how this reader searches: while it is happening to them, not before.
- **Why now:** discharge is one of the four trigger events named in `style/audience.md` ("a fall, a hospital discharge, a diagnosis, a spouse dying"). This is the only ECM post aimed squarely at that trigger. The one piece of adjacent coverage (post 212662, observation status) is thin, undated in structure, and does not cover the sequence.
- **Why this client:** the piece ends by routing the reader into exactly the categories ECM's directory sells: home care, assisted living, care management, elder law. It is neutral between settings, which is the house editorial line, because the directory lists all of them.

## Audience

The adult child persona from `style/audience.md`, specifically at the moment described there: "the decision is usually triggered by an event... they are on a deadline they did not choose." Here that event is a discharge date the hospital has already set, 24 to 72 hours out. They are reading on a phone, likely in a hospital corridor or after the parent is asleep, with ten minutes and one question: what do I do right now. They do not yet know whether their parent was "admitted" or "under observation," do not know a document called the Important Message from Medicare exists, and do not know Medicare home health is narrower than they assume. They need the sequence and the one lever they can pull if the timing feels wrong, not a glossary.

### Section 1: What Is the Hospital Required to Do Before My Parent Goes Home?

- Key point: hospitals must involve the patient and family as active partners, focus the plan on the patient's own goals, and hand over a list of Medicare-participating home health, rehab, and skilled nursing options serving the parent's area. The family can ask for this list and this choice by name.
- Evidence: research Finding 1, CMS discharge planning Conditions of Participation (finalized 2019). Do not cite a CFR section number, the pincite is unverified; describe the requirement in plain language only.
- Don't include: "facility," any CFR citation, any claim the hospital must accept a specific alternative setting.

### Section 2: Does It Matter If My Parent Was Admitted or Only Under Observation?

- Key point: yes, because only time as a formally admitted inpatient counts toward the days needed to qualify for a Medicare-covered rehab or skilled nursing stay. Time under observation does not count no matter how many nights it runs. State the 3-midnight structure as the current rule for Original Medicare.
- Evidence: research Finding 3 (structure only, no waiver detail) and Finding 4 (the MOON notice as the document that discloses observation status in writing).
- Treat this as one checkpoint, not a re-explanation. One or two sentences of substance, then hand the reader to the existing post via the internal link below.
- Link: `https://eldercarematters.com/medicare-observation-trap/`, anchor "the Medicare observation trap" (2 to 5 words).
- Don't include: the 2026-2030 surgical waiver demonstration, in any form, including "narrow exception" hedging language. Omit entirely per the orchestrator's instruction.

### Section 3: What Is the Important Message From Medicare, and Why Does It Matter Right Now?

- Key point: this is the one document and one phone call that can actually slow the process down. Describe narrowly: "a federal rule now lets some patients whose status was switched from inpatient to observation during the same stay appeal that change" is reserved for Section 4/5's appeal-right nuance, not this section. This section is about the IMM itself and the BFCC-QIO fast appeal generally available to Medicare inpatients who disagree with a discharge decision.
- Evidence: research Finding 2. State the mechanism (ask for the Important Message from Medicare, call the number on it to request a fast appeal with the area's BFCC-QIO, filing it pauses discharge while it is reviewed) without printing the exact day/hour timing numbers as hard facts, since medicare.gov could not be verified by direct fetch. Frame as "Medicare requires hospitals to give inpatients this notice on admission and again shortly before discharge" rather than citing the precise day counts.
- Don't include: the retrospective observation-status appeal-right detail (Barrows/Alexander), case names, the 2009 date range, Part B enrollment conditions. That nuance is too narrow and too unverified for this section; if mentioned anywhere, confine it to one sentence in the FAQ (see FAQ list below), worded exactly as the orchestrator specified.

### Section 4: What Can I Do If I Think My Parent Is Being Sent Home Too Soon?

- Key point: there is no separate veto power, but there is a real mechanism: tell the discharge planner in writing, use the hospital's grievance process, and for a Medicare inpatient, the BFCC-QIO fast appeal from Section 3 is the formal lever, because filing it keeps the patient in the hospital while it is reviewed.
- Evidence: research Finding 8. Ground this entirely in the CMS discharge planning requirement (grievance process, Section 1) and the BFCC-QIO appeal (Section 3). Do not cite CANHR, Disability Rights California, or any law-firm blog, and do not assert a legal duty not to discharge unsafely. Say what the family can do, not what the hospital is forbidden from doing.
- Don't include: any claim of a hospital's binding legal duty to avoid an unsafe discharge.

### Section 5: Where Can My Parent Go Next: Home, Rehab, or Skilled Nursing Care?

- Key point: there are a small number of real options and the right one depends on what the parent needs next, not on what sounds easiest today. Present the comparison table here (spec below).
- Evidence: synthesis of Findings 6 and 7, plus general post-acute landscape knowledge already in the research. Neutral tone, no setting is framed as the failure option.
- Links: `https://eldercarematters.com/assisted-living/` (anchor "assisted living communities") on the assisted-living/memory-care row; `https://eldercarematters.com/home-care-providers/` (anchor "home care providers") on the home-with-help row.
- Don't include: any claim that Medicare pays for assisted living, memory care, or custodial care in any table cell.

### Section 6: Will Medicare Pay for a Rehab or Skilled Nursing Stay?

- Key point: describe the structure only, never the dollar amounts: days 1 to 20 of a covered stay carry no coinsurance, days 21 to 100 carry a daily coinsurance amount that changes every year, and after day 100 Medicare pays nothing and the family pays in full. Tell the reader to confirm the current year's amount directly with Medicare.
- Also state: Medicare Advantage plans set their own skilled nursing coverage rules, and the plan's own documents control, so call the plan rather than assuming the same rule applies. Do not say MA plans "commonly waive" anything.
- Evidence: research Finding 6 (structure only) and the Finding 3 note on Medicare Advantage.
- Link: `https://eldercarematters.com/elder-law-attorneys/` (anchor "elder law attorneys"), on the sentence noting that Medicaid may pick up costs Medicare does not, without stating any figure, income cap, or look-back period.
- Don't include: $1,736, $217, any other dollar figure, any Medicaid asset limit or look-back detail.

### Section 7: What Will Medicare Home Health Actually Cover Once My Parent Is Home?

- Key point: Medicare home health is narrow. It requires the parent be homebound and need skilled nursing or therapy on an intermittent basis, under a doctor's plan of care. It does not cover 24-hour care, meals, shopping, cleaning, or personal care alone. This is the single most common and costly misunderstanding this audience has (per `style/audience.md`), so state it plainly and early in the section.
- Evidence: research Finding 7, NCOA, the strongest-sourced finding in the file, fetched directly and dated.
- External link: `https://www.ncoa.org/article/seven-things-you-should-know-about-medicares-home-health-care-benefit/`, `target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"`, anchor naming NCOA's home health article.
- Internal link: `https://eldercarematters.com/keeping-seniors-safe-at-home-fall-prevention-and-modifications/`, anchor "fall prevention and home modifications", on the sentence about what the family must arrange itself once the parent is home (safety, non-medical help).
- Internal link (second option, if word count allows): `https://eldercarematters.com/who-pays-for-respite-care/`, anchor "who pays for respite care", on a sentence acknowledging the family will be doing unpaid caregiving hours and may need a break.
- Don't include: any claim that home health substitutes for full-time care.

### Section 8: What Should I Ask the Discharge Planner Before My Parent Leaves?

- Numbered list (spec below), framed as questions the reader can ask in the next conversation with the discharge planner, whether by phone or in the room.
- Link: `https://eldercarematters.com/care-management/`, anchor "care management professionals", on a sentence noting a care manager can help coordinate answers to these questions if the family is not local or not able to be present.

### Section 9: What Should I Check Off Before My Parent Walks Out the Door?

### Close (Conclusion)

Answer the title question directly: sequence, not scramble. Restate in one sentence what the reader now has (the document to ask for, the appeal mechanism, the coverage structure, the settings comparison) and close by naming the next concrete action if care needs continue past the first weeks home: a home care provider, an assisted living community, or an elder law attorney, depending on what the parent actually needs. No hedging, no "every situation is different" trailing off.

## Comparison table spec

Title: "Where Your Parent Can Go After Discharge"

Columns: **Setting** | **Who It Usually Fits** | **What Medicare Covers** | **What Your Family Arranges**

Rows:
1. **Home, no added services** — a parent who is stable and largely independent — Nothing extra; ordinary follow-up care only — Everyday help, transportation, safety changes at home
2. **Home with Medicare home health** — a parent who needs short-term skilled nursing or therapy and is homebound — Skilled nursing or therapy visits, under a doctor's plan of care, on an intermittent basis — Everything outside that skilled visit: meals, bathing, supervision, 24-hour presence
3. **A rehab or skilled nursing stay** — a parent who needs daily skilled care or therapy after a qualifying inpatient hospital stay — A structured cost-sharing schedule that changes by day range (see Section 6); confirm current amounts with Medicare — Any stay beyond what Medicare covers, and next steps after discharge from that stay
4. **Assisted living or memory care** — a parent who needs regular help with daily activities but not hospital-level or skilled nursing care — Not covered by Medicare — The full cost, unless a state Medicaid program or other source applies; ask an elder law attorney about options

Row 4 must carry the `assisted-living` internal link. Row 2 must carry the `home-care-providers` internal link.

## Numbered discharge-planner questions (Section 8)

1. Has the hospital given us the required list of Medicare-participating home health, rehab, and skilled nursing options serving our area?
2. Was my parent formally admitted as an inpatient, or classified under observation, and for how many midnights?
3. Have we received the Important Message from Medicare, and do we have the phone number for our area's BFCC-QIO?
4. What specific skilled or therapy need would home health address, and who is the physician certifying the plan of care?
5. Does this hospital stay meet Medicare's admission requirement for a covered rehab or skilled nursing stay, if my parent needs one?
6. What has changed about my parent's ability to manage daily activities since before this hospital stay?
7. Who is our point of contact if a problem comes up in the first week after discharge?
8. What is the hospital's process if our family disagrees with the discharge plan?

## Checklist items (Section 9)

- We have the Important Message from Medicare and the BFCC-QIO phone number for our area
- We know whether my parent was admitted as an inpatient or held under observation
- We have the hospital's list of Medicare-participating home health, rehab, and skilled nursing options
- We understand what Medicare home health will and will not cover
- We have a plan for the first 24 hours home: medications, the follow-up appointment, and who will be present
- We have contacted an elder law attorney if Medicaid or longer-term planning questions have come up
- We have put any disagreement with the discharge plan in writing and given it to the discharge planner
