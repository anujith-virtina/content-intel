## 4. Target reader, and their state of mind

The adult child, 45 to 65, usually the sibling nearest the parent, often the one who took the call from the hospital. They have already concluded they cannot manage this alone. They are now about to spend real money on a professional they cannot evaluate.

**What they already know:** their parent's health in detail, that they are out of time, that they are behind.

**What they get wrong:** that "geriatric care manager" is a licensed profession. That letters after a name are self-explanatory. That belonging to a professional association is the same thing as holding a certification. That someone who recommends a community is neutral about which one.

**What they came for:** a way to tell a qualified professional from an expensive stranger, tonight, before tomorrow's phone call.

**Tone floor:** this post is skeptical about a job title, never about the people doing the job. Most care managers are licensed nurses and social workers doing careful work, and many of them are ECM's own paying listers. The post interrogates the market, not the person. ### Outline section 2: Is Geriatric Care Manager a Licensed Title? (185)

**This is the spine. Everything else in the post exists because of this section.**

Direct answer in the first sentence: no. There is no national license for geriatric care managers, and no governmental body regulates the title.

- Attribute inline to **The Tax Adviser, the journal of the American Institute of CPAs, June 2019**, which states that care managers are not regulated by any governmental entity and that anyone may use the title.
- **The balance, in the very next paragraph, and it is not optional.** The National Institute on Aging describes a geriatric care manager as usually a licensed nurse or social worker. Those underlying state licenses are regulated, carry disciplinary records, and can be revoked.
- **State both in the same breath.** The title is not regulated. The person underneath the title usually is. Both are true, and the combination is the reader's actual situation.
- The practical consequence, which is the whole post in one line: because the title tells you nothing, you have to ask what license and what certification sit underneath it.
- **Required phrasing guard.** Never write "geriatric care managers are unlicensed" or anything close to it. That sentence is false and it defames practising licensed professionals who are also this site's readers and listers. Say the **title** is not regulated.
- **Do not count states.** No 50-state survey of care manager title regulation exists. "No national license and no governmental body regulates the title" is the supported phrasing and the only phrasing. Never write "in 47 states", "in most states", or any number.

### Outline section 3: Which Care Manager Credentials Actually Mean Something? (280)

Direct answer: three certifications and two underlying professional licenses, and they certify completely different things.

**Prose before the table, about 45 words.** Two certifications are aging-specific and one is not. A state license is the stronger consumer protection, because a licensing board can revoke it and a voluntary certification body largely cannot.

**The table, about 175 words.** One table only in this post. Five columns, five rows plus a header. Mobile scroll wrapper, maroon `#660000` header, alternating `#f5f5f5` rows, same inline styling pattern as the most recent ECM draft. **Keep every cell to twelve words or fewer.**

| Credential | Issued By | What It Requires | Renewal | What It Does Not Tell You |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CMC, Care Manager Certified | National Academy of Certified Care Managers | Education plus paid, supervised care management work within the last ten years | Every three years, with continuing education | Anything about fees, business ownership, or referral payments |
| CCM, Certified Case Manager | Commission for Case Manager Certification | A health or human services license, or a related degree, plus case management experience | Every five years, 80 continuing education hours, 8 in ethics | Whether the holder has ever worked with older adults at home |
| C-SWCM and C-ASWCM | National Association of Social Workers | A social work degree, bachelor's level for C-SWCM, master's level for C-ASWCM | Confirm current terms directly with the issuing body | That it is a state license, because it is not |
| RN, registered nurse | Your parent's state nursing board | State nursing education and examination requirements | Set by each state board | Any specific training in care management |
| LCSW, licensed clinical social worker | Your parent's state social work board | A master's in social work plus supervised clinical hours | Set by each state board | Any specific training in care management |

**Prose after the table, about 60 words, carrying two points no competitor makes:**

1. **The CCM caveat, and it must appear.** CCM is a general case management credential held across health and human services. Many holders are hospital discharge planners or insurance case managers. A CCM may have spent an entire career in workers' compensation and never worked with an older adult in their home. Ask what the holder's case management experience was actually in.
2. **The CMC point, attributed to the National Academy of Certified Care Managers.** Its eligibility rules require paid, supervised care management experience within the last ten years, and the academy states plainly that a job title alone does not qualify a person. That is a meaningful filter.

**Spell every acronym on first use.** CMC, CCM, C-SWCM, C-ASWCM, RN, LCSW.

**Do not state whether the NASW credentials are currently being issued.** Name them, describe the bachelor's and master's distinction, say the Aging Life Care Association lists them among its qualifying certifications, and tell the reader to confirm current status with the issuer. Nothing more.

**Do not state that the CMC holds current accreditation** from any accrediting body. Unverified.

### Outline section 4: What Does Aging Life Care Association Membership Actually Tell You? (160)

Direct answer, first sentence: less than you think, because membership comes in levels and only the top one requires a certification at all.

- The three levels, attributed to the Aging Life Care Association in plain text. **Advanced Professional** requires a degree, years of supervised aging life care experience, **and** one of the qualifying certifications. **Professional** requires a degree and experience and **no certification**. **Associate** is the entry level, open on a degree, a nursing diploma, or an LPN, PTA, or OTA license plus a paid role in the field.
- **The line that does the work:** a family told only "they are an ALCA member" has learned almost nothing. The question is at what level, and which certification qualified them.
- Scale, attributed to the Aging Life Care Association, 29 April 2026: the association formed in 1985 and has nearly 2,000 members nationwide.
- **The fairness sentence, required.** Two thousand members across the whole country means many capable care managers are not members and many areas have none nearby. Membership is a useful signal and a complaint route, not a license and not a requirement.
- Spell out ALCA, LPN, PTA, and OTA on first use.
- **Do not link aginglifecare.org.** It returns HTTP 403 to automated requests and cannot be verified. Name the association in plain text and give its public phone number, 520-881-8008.

### Outline section 5: How Do You Check That a Credential Is Real? (165)

Direct answer: every claim in section 3 is checkable by you, free, in about five minutes, and the check is on the issuing body's own site rather than the care manager's.

Short numbered list, five items, each one line of instruction and one line on what it returns.

1. **A nursing license.** Nursys, the national nurse license verification service run by the National Council of State Boards of Nursing, is free to the public and returns license status and publicly available discipline for participating boards.
2. **A clinical social work license.** There is no national database. Go to the state social work board's own lookup. The Association of Social Work Boards points to each state's. Expect status, license type, expiry, and disciplinary actions in most states.
3. **A CMC.** The National Academy of Certified Care Managers takes verification requests through a form on its site and answers within a few business days.
4. **A CCM.** The Commission for Case Manager Certification runs a public verification lookup.
5. **ALCA membership.** Use the association's own member search and read the **level**, which is the part that matters.

**Closing line, carrying internal link 1:** once the credentials check out, search `care management professionals` serving your parent's area.

**Do not promise a turnaround time in hours**, and do not state a response-time guarantee for any of these.

### Outline section 6: Who Is Actually Paying Your Care Manager? (215)

**This is the differentiator. No competing page raises it.**

Direct answer, first two sentences: most care managers are paid only by the family, but some also own or are paid by businesses they could recommend, and the only way to know which is sitting across the table is to ask and to get the answer in writing.

- **The ethics standard, attributed to the Aging Life Care Association Code of Ethics and Standards of Practice, revised June 2025.** It requires full disclosure of business, professional, or personal relationships when making a referral. It requires that any separate business taking or offering referral fees be operated independently from the aging life care practice. It advises avoiding arrangements where the professional pays themselves from a client's funds, and recommends third-party oversight where that cannot be avoided. **Paraphrase, do not quote.** The full document is member-gated and every clause reached us through a single channel.
- **The remedy, and it only exists for members.** The association operates a Professional Conduct Review Committee and publishes a complaint form for formal allegations that a member breached the code. A care manager who belongs to no association has no such body behind them. Say that plainly and without implying non-members behave worse.
- **The wider referral fee problem, with legislation, stated neutrally.** Some businesses in senior care are paid a fee by a provider when a family moves in. Legislatures have started requiring that this be disclosed. **Georgia Senate Bill 439**, effective **1 July 2026**, requires referral agencies working with assisted living and personal care homes to disclose how they are paid, that they collect a fee on a successful move-in, and that families may have options beyond what the agency shows them. **Washington** did it first, in 2011, requiring upfront fee disclosure and at least $1 million in liability insurance.
- **The distinction the section must draw, in its own sentence.** These laws regulate referral agencies, not care managers. A care manager paid only by your family collects no referral fee. A care manager who also runs or is paid by a referral business does.
- **The reader's action, concrete:** ask directly whether they receive any payment from any `home care providers` or `assisted living communities` they recommend, and ask for the answer in the engagement agreement. Internal links 3 and 4 land here.
- **Use "referral agency" throughout**, which is also the term the Georgia statute uses. "Placement" is banned.
- **Never accuse any category of business of taking kickbacks.** Disclosure laws exist because disclosure was absent, not because fraud is universal. **Never name a blocked competitor**, even as an example of the business model.

### Outline section 7: What Does a Geriatric Care Manager Cost? (190)

Direct answer: expect to pay by the hour, and expect the first assessment to be the biggest single bill.

- **The only figures in this post**, attributed inline to **AARP, updated 5 August 2026**: $150 to $300 an hour depending on region, and an initial assessment that typically runs 4 to 6 hours of billable time. AARP also notes some care managers bill mileage and travel time, and advises getting the fee structure in writing before you agree to anything. **External link 2 goes here.**
- **Attribute AARP twice in this section**, once at the rate and once at the assessment hours, so a reader skimming either paragraph sees the source.
- The sharp interview question, credited to The Tax Adviser, June 2019, as guidance and not as a cost claim: ask what billing increment is used, 6, 10, or 15 minutes, because the increment changes the bill more than the rate does.
- **Who pays, attributed to the National Institute on Aging in plain text:** Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for geriatric care management, most private health insurance does not, most families pay out of pocket, and long-term care insurance may cover part of it.
- **One addition from AARP, updated 5 August 2026:** some employee assistance programs cover part of care management fees, and Medigap and Medicare Advantage plans do not. Spell out the term the first time.
- **The free action, one sentence:** call your own employer's benefits line and ask whether the employee assistance program covers care management.
- **Mandatory routing sentence, carrying internal link 2:** if Medicaid planning comes up in the conversation, that is a legal and financial question that varies by state, so take it to an `elder law attorney`.
- **Do not link medicare.gov, medicaid.gov, or cms.gov.** Name them in plain text only.

### Outline section 8: Questions to Ask Before You Hire a Care Manager (185)

One setup line: ask these on the first call, write the answers down, and notice which ones cause hesitation.

Numbered list, exactly twelve. **Average fourteen words or fewer per question.** The independence block, items 9 through 12, is the reason this list exists and none of the four may be merged or cut.

1. What license do you hold, in which state, and what is the license number?
2. Which care management certification do you hold, and when does it renew?
3. If you belong to the Aging Life Care Association, at what level, and which certification qualified you?
4. How many active clients are you carrying right now?
5. Who covers your clients when you are away, ill, or unreachable?
6. What is your after-hours and weekend availability, and what counts as an emergency?
7. What is your hourly rate, what billing increment do you use, and do you bill mileage, travel, and phone calls?
8. How many hours will the assessment take, what do we receive afterward, and will the fee agreement be in writing first?
9. Do you, your spouse, or your company hold any ownership in, or financial relationship with, any home care agency, senior living community, or referral agency?
10. Do you ever receive a referral fee, commission, or any other payment from a provider you recommend?
11. Will you put that answer in writing in the engagement agreement?
12. If we do not take your recommendation, does anything change about what you charge us?

**Two one-clause notes the creator should add, and no more than two:** items 4 through 6 are the questions almost nobody asks, and item 11 is the one that turns a verbal reassurance into something enforceable.

**Do not supply a benchmark caseload number.** No authority publishes one. The value of item 4 is the answer plus item 5, not a target figure.

### Outline section 9: What Are the Warning Signs You Should Walk Away? (115)

Direct answer: hesitation on any question about money, coverage, or independence.

Short list, eight items, each one line. Every item traces to something earlier in the post.

- Will not give a license, a state, and a license number
- Cannot name which certification they hold, or names one no recognised body issues
- Refuses to put fees in writing, or the agreement omits mileage, travel, and phone time
- Cannot say who covers the account when they are away
- Is paid by, or owns part of, a provider they recommend, and did not volunteer it
- Recommends a specific community on the first call, before any assessment
- Promises a specific outcome, a wait list bypass, or a Medicaid result
- Asks to be paid from accounts they themselves manage for your parent

**Frame these as reasons to keep looking, never as accusations.** No sentence in this section may assert that any of this is common.

### Outline section 10: Do You Actually Need a Paid Care Manager? (145)

**The honesty section. It is the reason ECM can outrank a care management practice's own blog, because ECM does not sell care management.**

Direct answer: not always, and paid care management earns its fee mainly where distance, complexity, family conflict, or your own time is the binding constraint.

- The three sourced framings, kept light: the National Institute on Aging describes care managers as especially helpful when a caregiver lives far away, and AARP frames the value partly around a working caregiver's time. Post 274329 covers `when shared caregiving isn't enough`, internal link 6.
- **The plain statement:** if you live nearby, your parent's needs are stable and simple, your family agrees, and you have the hours, you may not need to pay for this at all.
- **The free first call, carrying external link 1.** Your Area Agency on Aging offers options counseling and care coordination, and the `Eldercare Locator` connects you to the one serving your parent's address. Attribute to the Administration for Community Living in plain text.
- **Required limitation, same paragraph:** services vary by area, many have waiting lists, and some are targeted by income. Do not promise anyone free care management.
- **The bounded purchase, one sentence:** a care manager can be bought in hours rather than by the year, and a one-time assessment with a written plan is a legitimate stopping point.
- Carry internal link 7 on the time and exhaustion point: `caregiver burnout`.

### Outline section 11: Check These Before You Sign the Agreement (80)

Checklist, eight items, about ten words each. One setup line: none of this takes long, and all of it is easier before money changes hands.

- A written fee agreement naming the rate, the billing increment, and travel charges
- The license number confirmed on the state board's own lookup, not theirs
- The certification confirmed with the issuing body, not with the care manager
- The association membership level confirmed, not just the membership
- A written answer on referral fees and any ownership interest
- Two references from families served longer than six months
- Agreement in writing on what the assessment delivers, and by when
- A named backup for vacations, illness, and after-hours calls

### Outline section 12: People Also Ask (105)

Flat H3s, no accordion. Three questions, each answered in two to four sentences, direct answer first. None repeats a body H2.

1. **`What Is the Difference Between a Geriatric Care Manager and an Aging Life Care Professional?`** They describe the same work. "Aging life care professional" is the term the Aging Life Care Association uses for its members, and "geriatric care manager" is the older and more widely searched name. Some practitioners use "care manager" alone. The words do not tell you anything about qualifications, which is why the certifications matter.
2. **`Who Does a Geriatric Care Manager Work For, Your Parent or You?`** Usually whoever signs the agreement and pays, and that should be written down before work starts. Ask who is named as the client, who receives the reports, and what happens if your parent and you disagree. Settle it in the engagement agreement rather than in the first crisis.
3. **`Can a Care Manager Help If You Live in Another State?`** Yes, and the National Institute on Aging describes this as one of the situations where a care manager is most useful. Long-distance families should press hardest on the coverage questions, because you cannot drop in. Confirm reporting format, frequency, and who else gets copied.

### Outline section 14: Frequently Asked Questions (190)

Six questions, `<details>` accordion, `<h3 class="ecm-q">` kept **inside** `<summary>`, plus and minus icons in `#660000`, scoped `<style>` block where **every typography rule carries `!important` and includes `.single-post .entry-content` in the selector**. Copy the exact accordion markup and CSS from the most recent ECM draft. FAQ type at 15px, never lower.

None of these restates body copy.

1. **`Can You Hire a Care Manager for Just One Assessment?`** Usually yes. A one-time assessment and a written plan is a normal, bounded engagement, and you are not obliged to buy ongoing management. Ask what the assessment includes and what the written plan looks like before you agree.
2. **`Does Long-Term Care Insurance Pay for Care Management?`** It may cover part of it, according to the National Institute on Aging, and it depends entirely on the policy. Read the policy language or ask the insurer in writing before you count on it. Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for this service.
3. **`Could Your Employer's Benefits Cover Part of the Fee?`** Sometimes. AARP, in guidance updated 5 August 2026, notes that some employee assistance programs cover part of care management fees, because the service helps a working caregiver stay at work. It costs one phone call to your own benefits line to find out.
4. **`What Should You Gather Before the First Meeting?`** A current medication list, insurance cards, any recent hospital discharge paperwork, and copies of legal documents such as a power of attorney. Gathering these first keeps billable hours from being spent on paperwork you could have found. Ask the care manager what else they want in advance.
5. **`What Can You Do If the Working Relationship Goes Wrong?`** End it according to the notice terms in your agreement, which is another reason to have one. If the care manager holds a state nursing or social work license, the state board takes complaints about that license. If they belong to the Aging Life Care Association, its conduct review process is open to you as well.
6. **`Can a Care Manager Make Decisions for Your Parent?`** No. Hiring one gives them no legal authority. Decision-making authority comes from documents such as a power of attorney, often abbreviated POA, or a health care proxy, and those are prepared with an elder law attorney.

## 8. Do not publish, ever
