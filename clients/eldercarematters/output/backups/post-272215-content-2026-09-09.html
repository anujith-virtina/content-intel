Introduction

Respite care for family caregivers is short-term care that somebody else provides so you can step away. It is a service you can price, book, and repeat.

Most caregivers never use it. In the focus groups AARP ran in 2025, many had never been told it existed.

AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving counted 63 million family caregivers in the United States. That figure comes from Caregiving in the U.S. 2025, released in July 2025.

This guide covers the four forms respite takes, what each costs, who might pay, and how to arrange it.

What Is Respite Care?

Respite care is planned short-term care that stands in for you. It can run for a few hours, a weekend, or a couple of weeks.

You choose the setting, the hours, and the tasks. Your parent's routine carries on while you are away.

What a Respite Worker Does

Most respite covers the same ground you cover: personal care, medication reminders, help moving safely, meals, and company.

What is allowed varies by provider and by state. Ask whether the worker can give medication, drive, or help with transfers.

How Respite Is Sold and Booked

In-home visits are sold in blocks of hours, with a minimum length per visit. Short stays are sold by the day.

Almost none of it is same-day. Intake and assessment come first, so look into respite before you need it.

The Four Main Types of Respite Care

Respite takes four practical forms. They differ in setting, length, and cost.

Type Where It Happens Typical Length Median Cost
In-home respite An aide or companion at home A few hours to overnight $35 an hour
Adult day program A licensed day center 7 to 10 hours, weekdays $95 a day
Short-term residential respite A room in an assisted living or nursing community Several days to a few weeks No national median is published. Ask for the community's daily rate
Informal or volunteer respite Home, or a community setting A few hours at a time Free or a small fee

Both dollar figures are national medians from the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, fielded July through November 2025 and released in March 2026.

In-Home Respite

A paid aide or companion comes to the house so you can leave, which helps if your parent is unsettled by new places.

Agencies sell everything from company to personal care and skilled nursing, and most families buy it from home care providers.

Adult Day Programs

Your parent attends a licensed day center on weekdays, with meals, activities, and supervision. It is the lowest cost per hour of any paid respite.

Our adult day care guide covers the program models and how to enroll.

Short-Term Residential Respite

Your parent stays in an assisted living community, a memory care community, or a nursing community for a short defined stay.

This is the option for caregiver travel, surgery, or recovery, and some families use it as a trial run before a move.

Informal and Volunteer Respite

Family, friends, neighbors, and volunteer programs cover hours free or for a small fee. If your sister already sits with your mother on Thursdays, that is respite. A written rota makes it dependable.

How Much Does Respite Care Cost?

An aide at home has a national median rate of $35 an hour. Adult day health care has a national median of $95 a day.

Both come from the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, fielded July through November 2025 and released in March 2026. Both are medians, not averages.

What In-Home Respite Costs

At that 2025 median of $35 an hour, a four-hour block twice a week costs about $280 a week.

Skilled nursing at home is a different service, at a median of $90 an hour in the same survey.

What an Adult Day Program Costs

The CareScout 2025 median for adult day health care is $95 a day. Attending five days a week works out at about $24,700 a year.

Why Short Stays Are Harder to Price

No approved national source publishes a median for short-term residential respite. Any national figure you see is derived from something else.

Each community sets its own daily rate, often higher than a permanent resident pays. Ask for the daily respite rate, what it includes, and the minimum stay.

Who Pays for Respite Care?

Most families start by paying privately. Four other payers exist, and each has narrow rules.

Payer What It Covers What to Know
Medicare Nothing for ongoing custodial respite, except inpatient respite under the hospice benefit Only if your parent is already on hospice. CMS: up to 5 consecutive days at a time
Medicaid HCBS waivers Respite is a covered service under 1915(c) waivers in many states Rules and waiting lists vary by state. Eligibility is a question for an elder law attorney
VA Respite for enrolled veterans at home, in adult day programs, and in inpatient settings A copay may apply. Ask the VA social worker at your parent's VA medical center
Older Americans Act funding Respite is one of the funded caregiver service categories Reached through your Area Agency on Aging, via the Eldercare Locator
Private pay Every form of respite, at the rates above How most families start. Ask about sliding scales and cancellation terms

What Medicare Covers, and What It Does Not

Medicare does not pay for ongoing custodial respite care. It will not cover an aide who sits with your parent so you can sleep.

There is one narrow exception. If your parent has elected the Medicare hospice benefit, it includes short-term inpatient respite so you can rest.

CMS, on a page dated 2 July 2026, describes up to five consecutive days at a time in an approved inpatient setting. Coinsurance is 5% of the Medicare payment for the respite stay, capped at that year's inpatient hospital deductible.

This is a hospice benefit, not a general one. See our explainer on how hospice care works.

Medicaid and Home and Community Based Waivers

Respite is a covered service under Medicaid 1915(c) home and community based services waivers, usually shortened to HCBS waivers, in many states.

What is covered and whether there is a waiting list vary by state. Eligibility is a legal and financial question, so speak to an elder law attorney before you move any money.

Respite Care for Veterans

The VA offers respite to enrolled veterans who meet its clinical criteria, at home, through adult day programs, and in inpatient settings.

A copay may apply, and how the VA counts a respite day differs by setting. Ask the VA social worker at your parent's VA medical center.

Help Through Your Area Agency on Aging

The Older Americans Act funds caregiver support through the states, and respite is one of the funded categories. You reach it through your local Area Agency on Aging.

Paying Privately

Most respite is paid out of pocket, so ask about sliding scales, scholarships, and your state's respite coalition.

A long-term care policy may cover respite if it names the service. Tax deductibility is a question for a tax professional.

How Respite Care Helps You, Not Just Your Parent

A break does the obvious things. You sleep, you keep your own medical appointments, and you get hours that belong to you.

Time apart also eases the relationship, and your parent gains new company and a second set of eyes.

The strongest case is the recurring break, already in the calendar, so relief does not wait for a crisis. Our guide to avoiding caregiver burnout covers the rest.

Why Caregivers Put Off Asking for Help

Very few caregivers use formal respite, and the reasons are consistent. AARP Research published findings from nine caregiver focus groups on 21 November 2025.

The Four Reasons Caregivers Name

Guilt and duty came first. Caregivers described asking for help as stepping away from something that is theirs to do. AARP noted those expectations run especially deep in Black, Hispanic, and Asian families.

Cost came second, described as expensive for regular full-day use. Trust came third, because handing your mother to a stranger is a real concern.

The fourth reason is the quietest. Most did not know formal respite existed, so they relied on family and friends.

What Caregivers Said Would Change Their Minds

Two answers stand out because you can act on both. The first is consistent staffing, so the same person comes each time.

The second is hearing about respite early, before a crisis rather than during one.

In Caregiving in the U.S. 2025, almost 40% of caregivers said respite services would be helpful to them.

How to Arrange Respite Care, Step by Step

This is a week's work rather than an afternoon's.

  1. Write down what you need: hours, tasks, setting, and budget.
  2. Call the Eldercare Locator on 1-800-677-1116 and ask for your Area Agency on Aging. The Locator is a free public service of the Administration for Community Living.
  3. Ask what respite your state funds, how long the wait is, and whether a sliding scale applies.
  4. If your parent is a veteran, ask their VA social worker about respite.
  5. If your parent is on hospice, ask the hospice team about inpatient respite.
  6. Shortlist two or three providers, ask the questions below, and book a short trial first.

Questions to Ask a Respite Provider

Take these to an agency call or a community tour. Written answers let you compare providers honestly.

  1. Will the same person come each time?
  2. What training does your staff have, and is it dementia specific?
  3. What is your minimum visit or minimum stay?
  4. How do you report back, and who calls me if something changes?
  5. How do cancellations work, at your end and mine?
  6. What is the rate, and what is not included in it?
  7. Can we start with one short trial visit?
  8. How far ahead must I book, and can you cover an emergency?

Question one matters more than families expect. Consistency is what caregivers told AARP would build trust.

What to Prepare Before You Hand Over Care

A substitute can only do the job if they know what you know.

  • A current medication list, with doses and times
  • The doctor's name and number, and the rest of the medical team
  • The daily routine in order, including sleep and mealtimes
  • What helps when your parent is anxious, and what makes it worse
  • What your parent can do alone, and what needs a second pair of hands
  • Allergies, dietary needs, and the foods they will not touch
  • Where things are kept: keys, chargers, supplies, spare hearing aid batteries
  • Who to call in an emergency, in order, including a named backup

Do the first handover in a calm week rather than a crisis. First visits rarely go perfectly.

Use This Respite Readiness Checklist

Work through this before you book anything.

  • The hours you need covered, split into days and nights
  • The form of respite that fits those hours
  • Written rates from two providers, including what is not in the rate
  • Whether your state's Medicaid waiver covers respite, checked with an elder law attorney
  • Whether your parent qualifies for VA respite, checked with their VA social worker
  • The next three respite dates in the calendar, not only the first

People Also Ask

What Is the Difference Between Respite Care and Home Care?

The tasks overlap, but the purpose differs. Home care is ongoing support for your parent, while respite is cover so you can be elsewhere.

How Far in Advance Should I Book Respite Care?

Further ahead than feels necessary. Intake and assessment take time, short stays fill up around holidays, and state-funded respite can carry a waiting list.

What If My Parent Does Not Want Anyone Else There?

That reaction is common and rarely permanent. Start with a short visit while you are still in the house, and introduce the worker as help for you.

Conclusion

Respite care is a service with a price, a provider, and a booking date. It is not a reward for coping well enough for long enough.

Start with the smallest version that helps: one recurring block a week, in the setting your parent tolerates best.

If you cannot work out where to begin, care managers assess the situation and put a plan in writing. To find providers near your parent, call the Eldercare Locator on 1-800-677-1116.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is There a Minimum Number of Hours for In-Home Respite?

Usually yes, and some agencies set a weekly minimum too. Ask before comparing hourly rates, because a four-hour minimum can raise the real cost.

Is Respite Care Only for Families Living With Dementia?

No. Respite is defined by who needs the break, not by a diagnosis. Families managing frailty, mobility loss, or recovery also use it.

Can I Arrange Respite Care in an Emergency?

Sometimes, but do not build a plan on it. Some agencies and state programs keep emergency capacity, so ask now and write the answer down.

Does Long-Term Care Insurance Pay for Respite Care?

It can, if the policy names respite as a covered service. Policies differ on triggers and daily limits, so ask the insurer to confirm in writing.

What If the First Respite Visit Does Not Go Well?

Treat it as information rather than a verdict. Tell the agency what did not work, ask for a different worker, and try a shorter slot.

Can Siblings Split the Cost of Respite Care?

Often, and putting a number on the hours makes the conversation easier. A written rate and a written schedule turn a vague request into a specific one.

Should I Tell My Parent That This Is Respite Care?

Use whatever language lands well at home. Many families call it a visitor or a standing appointment, as long as your parent knows who is coming.
