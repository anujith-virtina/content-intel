# Your first B2B store doesn't have to be your final one

Most manufacturers stall on the same question. They look at everything a B2B store could do and decide the project is too big to start.

It isn't. The stores that work usually started small, with a few buyers and one clear job.

## Why does a manufacturer's online store feel so big?

Because manufacturers don't sell like retailers.

Prices differ by customer. Many orders begin as a quote request. Account data lives in an ERP. A store that ignores those three facts looks fine on launch day and fails on the first real order.

So the wish list grows. Customer pricing, quotes, reorders, approvals, ERP sync, account permissions. Put it all in one launch plan and the project never leaves the planning stage.

## What should you build first?

Build for the buyers you already have.

Start with the accounts that reorder often and the parts they buy most. Those buyers already know you. They don't need convincing. They need an easier way to place the same order again.

That one job, a fast reorder with their own pricing, is a complete first release.

## How do you roll it out without breaking anything?

Use four steps:

1. Map your current buyers, products, and pricing rules.
2. Run a pilot with a few key accounts.
3. Add quotes, reorders, and integrations in phases.
4. Train your sales team and measure adoption.

The order matters. The pilot teaches you what the map missed. Phases keep each change small enough to test. Training comes before the full rollout, so your reps see the store as help, not a threat.

Picture a manufacturer with 4,000 parts and 300 active accounts. This is an illustration, not a client result. It would start with twenty large accounts. Routine orders move online first. Custom parts still go to a person.

## How should quotes and payment terms work online?

Quotes can come in by form or chat. Standard items can get an instant answer. Custom parts go to a person on your team.

Net terms need more care. A buyer should only see them after a credit check and approval. Showing terms to everyone creates risk you can't take back.

## Which platform fits a manufacturer?

It depends on the catalog, the ERP, and how much custom pricing you run. The common choices are WooCommerce, Adobe Commerce, BigCommerce, and Shopify Plus. Each fits a different shape of business.

Choose after you've mapped your buyers and pricing rules, not before. The map decides what the platform has to do.

## What does a good result look like?

Results vary by business, and a store is only one part of any result. Two of Virtina's clients have shared figures on virtina.com. These are client-reported.

Robert Rinker, Founder of MM-Source, reported 22.5% revenue growth in nine months. Paul Rhoads, Vice President at Energy Pricing, reported a 7.1% conversion rate, against a 2.5% industry average.

Measure your own store on five things: online order share, average order value, repeat order rate, time to quote, and support calls avoided. If those move, the store is working.

## What's the takeaway?

You don't have to rebuild everything at once. Start with the buyers and processes you already have. Add features in phases.
