## Key findings

### Finding 1: Reps report spending well under a third of their week actually selling

- Source: [New Research Reveals Sales Reps Need a Productivity Overhaul](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/sales-research-2023/) — Salesforce Newsroom, fifth edition "State of Sales" report
- What it says: 7,775 sales professionals surveyed across North America, Latin America, Asia-Pacific and Europe (fielded Aug 24–Sept 30, 2022) reported spending 28% of their week actually selling. The rest goes to deal management, data entry, and administrative work.
- Why it matters: this is the plainest, most defensible entry point for the piece — a named, dated, large-sample survey from a company with no reason to inflate the non-selling share. It grounds the "what does a rep's day actually look like" section before ChatSKU is mentioned at all.

### Finding 2: Sales-org leaders themselves frame AI as removing routine work, not people

- Source: [Why AI will elevate instead of replace commercial teams](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/why-ai-will-elevate-instead-of-replace-commercial-teams) — McKinsey, published July 28, 2026 (interview with Covestro's chief commercial officer)
- What it says: the interview frames AI's role as removing routine work so commercial teams can focus on relationships, cross-functional collaboration and value creation, not as a headcount play.
- Why it matters: a credible, named executive (not a vendor) making the augmentation case in her own words is more persuasive than another vendor blog saying the same thing. ### Finding 3: Where headcount actually is shrinking, it is concentrated in junior, high-volume, low-complexity roles — and it is real, not hype

- Source: [The Great SDR Downsizing: 36% of B2B Companies Cut Sales Development Teams in 2025](https://www.saastr.com/the-great-sdr-downsizing-36-of-b2b-companies-cut-sales-development-teams-in-2025/) — SaaStr, citing Emergence Capital's "Beyond Benchmarks" survey (with Benchmarkit), fielded April 2025, 560+ venture-backed B2B software companies
- What it says: 36% of companies decreased SDR/BDR headcount in the prior 12 months (the highest cut rate of any sales function measured), 19% increased it, 44% held flat. Account executives were cut at 25%, sales engineers at 14% (the lowest).
- Why it matters: this is the honest counterweight the brief demands. It is real, named, and dated — not a whitewash. **Important caveat to carry into the draft**: this sample is venture-backed B2B *software* companies, not manufacturers or distributors. A separate, secondary citation trail (via search-aggregated summaries, not independently verified against a primary Prospeo document) claims the same 2026 data splits as 18% net SDR headcount decline, junior roles down 31%, senior roles up 14% — **flag this second figure set as unverified**, since it could not be traced to a primary document and shares its ultimate source chain with the Emergence Capital figure once aggregator sites are stripped out.

### Finding 4: Ramp time for a new B2B sales hire is measured in months, not weeks, and it has been getting longer, not shorter

- Source: [Latest SDR Metrics & Compensation Research](https://blog.bridgegroupinc.com/sales-development-metrics) and related Bridge Group reporting — The Bridge Group, a B2B sales research firm; SDR data drawn from 351 B2B companies (2025 fielding); AE ramp data trend reported across the firm's annual SaaS AE Metrics reports
- What it says: average SDR ramp time is roughly 3.2 months; average account-executive ramp time has been rising across recent editions (reported around 4.3 months in 2020, 5.3 in 2022, 5.7 in more recent editions).
- Why it matters: this supports the "what changes for a brand-new rep" section — a new hire spends months learning where things are and what the standard answers are before they generate real pipeline. **Caveat**: Bridge Group's sample is SaaS sales organizations, not physical-goods distribution or manufacturing sales. The article should state this plainly rather than borrow the SaaS number as if it were a distributor number — use it as an order-of-magnitude reference ("measured in months across B2B sales roles generally") rather than a precise distributor figure.

### Finding 5: Forrester's own job-impact forecast is a genuinely balanced source, not doom or reassurance

- Source: [Forrester: AI-Led Job Disruption Will Escalate, While Fears Of A Job Apocalypse Are Overstated](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-impact-ai-jobs-forecast/) — Forrester press release
- What it says: forecasts 6% of total US jobs automated by 2030 (about 10.4 million roles) and roughly 20% of jobs augmented rather than eliminated over five years. Customer-facing and junior roles feel disproportionate pressure. Notably, Forrester states more than half of AI-attributed layoffs get "quietly reversed" once companies discover the operational cost of removing people prematurely.
- Why it matters: this is the single best source for the article's honesty section, because it is explicitly not a vendor and it argues against both extremes (no job loss / total replacement). The "layoffs quietly reversed" finding is a genuinely fresh, non-reassuring-sounding data point that supports the augmentation thesis without sounding like spin.

### Finding 6: What tasks are broadly agreed to still need a human, even by sources arguing AI will shrink some roles

- Source: [Where AI will and won't replace sales reps in 2026](https://www.saastr.com/where-ai-will-and-wont-replace-sales-reps-in-2026/) — SaaStr (Jason Lemkin)
- What it says: names three areas that stay human even as AI absorbs routine and transactional work: strategic relationship-building on larger accounts, navigating multi-stakeholder procurement, and creative problem-solving on unique customer situations. Also frames AI as most useful against "one-call closes" and highly structured, repetitive tasks.
- Why it matters: this maps cleanly onto ChatSKU's own positioning (negotiation, complex configuration, exceptions, judgment calls stay human) from an independent, non-vendor voice in B2B sales commentary, which strengthens credibility for the "what a catalog assistant genuinely cannot do" section.

## Conflicts and disagreements

- **Position A (Forrester, McKinsey, SaaStr's own hybrid framing):** AI augments B2B sales; role composition shifts toward senior, relationship-heavy work; junior/transactional work shrinks but does not disappear from the org overall.
- **Position B (Emergence Capital "Beyond Benchmarks" data, read plainly):** actual headcount reduction is happening now, concentrated in SDR/BDR roles (36% of companies cut them in the prior year), and account executive roles were also cut at a meaningful rate (25%).
- **What's actually true:** both are true at once, and the honest framing is: the roles most likely to shrink are the ones doing the most repetitive, high-volume, low-complexity work — exactly the tasks a catalog assistant also targets. That is uncomfortable for a "no jobs lost" pitch, and the article should say so plainly rather than soften it, while noting the specific data (Emergence Capital) describes venture-backed SaaS software companies, a different world from a $5M-$50M physical-goods distributor with a small, tenured inside sales team. The honest read for ChatSKU's actual audience: distributors are not laying off inside sales reps over a catalog assistant; the SaaS SDR-army model that is shrinking does not describe how most $1M-$50M manufacturers/distributors staff sales in the first place (they typically have 1-8 inside reps, not a 20-person SDR pod running high-volume outbound).

## 6 facts the article must carry

1. Reps report spending under a third of their week on activities that actually move a deal forward; the rest is repetitive lookups, quotes, and admin (Salesforce State of Sales, 28%/72% split, n=7,775, 2022 field/2023 publish).
2. Where AI is measurably shrinking sales headcount today, the cuts concentrate in the most repetitive, highest-volume, lowest-complexity roles (SDR/BDR cut at 36% vs. account executives at 25% vs. sales engineers at 14%, Emergence Capital "Beyond Benchmarks," April 2025, n=560+ B2B software companies) — and this pattern does not map cleanly onto a small distributor's 1-8-person inside sales team, which should be stated honestly rather than glossed over.
3. New hires in B2B sales roles take months, not weeks, to reach full productivity (SDR ~3.2 months; AE ramp climbing across recent years to ~5.7 months, Bridge Group data) — most of that time is spent learning where answers live, not learning to sell.
4. Independent, non-vendor forecasting (Forrester) puts total US job automation at 6% by 2030 with roughly 20% of jobs augmented rather than eliminated, and notes over half of AI-attributed layoffs get quietly reversed once the operational cost of losing people becomes clear — a genuinely balanced, non-vendor data point.
5. Even sources willing to say AI is replacing parts of sales work agree the same three things stay human: relationship-building on bigger or more complex accounts, navigating multiple stakeholders in a purchase decision, and judgment calls on situations that do not fit a standard pattern (SaaStr/Lemkin commentary, 2026).
6. The failure modes for a bad rollout are consistent across independent reporting on sales-tool adoption generally: reps route around tools they do not trust when the underlying data is unreliable, and leads captured by an automated front end die quietly when no one owns the handoff to a human. (These patterns are directional and drawn from multiple secondary vendor/practitioner sources rather than one named study; present as consensus practitioner experience, not as a cited statistic.)

## Honest downside material (for the "what goes wrong" section)

- **Reps route around tools they don't trust.** The recurring pattern across sales-ops commentary is that adoption fails when the underlying data is unreliable — reps develop informal workarounds (their own notes, spreadsheets, unofficial tools) rather than rely on a system that has burned them once. The mechanism, not a specific number, is what's citable here: trust breaks the first time the assistant gives a wrong stock count or a stale price, and it is slow to rebuild.
- **Nobody owns the handoff.** A widely repeated practitioner observation (not a single verifiable study) is that an assistant capturing a lead and a human following up are two different systems, and if nobody is explicitly responsible for picking up what the assistant hands off, the lead goes cold exactly as fast as it would have in a shared inbox nobody checks. This is an organizational failure, not a technology failure, and the article should say so.
- **A manager who doesn't change what they measure gets no benefit.** If a sales manager keeps measuring "calls made" and "emails sent" after routine questions move to a catalog assistant, reps have no signal to spend the freed time on judgment work instead — the freed hours quietly get eaten by lower-value busywork rather than reallocated to relationship building. This is inference from the augmentation research (McKinsey, SaaStr) rather than a distinct study, and should be framed as an operational observation, not a stat.
- **New hires can hide behind the assistant instead of learning the catalog.** This is a plausible, named risk worth stating honestly even though no study measures it directly: if a brand-new rep never has to learn where things live because the assistant always has the answer, they may take longer to develop the judgment for the harder calls that do land on them. Flag as a reasoned risk, not a documented finding.
