<h2>Executive summary</h2>
<p>Once an AI catalog assistant takes over first-touch spec, price, and availability questions, three parts of how you run a sales team go stale: what you measure, what you pay for, and what you teach a new hire in month one. Not because the team shrank. Because the job changed and nobody updated the paperwork.</p>
<p>The fix has three parts, one for each lever. Stop scoring reps on activity the assistant now performs, and build scorecards around deflection rate, qualified-handoff rate, and time-to-quota instead. Stop paying commission on the first quote sent, and reset the trigger to the close, the exception, the save. Stop spending the first month of ramp memorizing a price sheet the assistant already holds, and spend it instead on judgment calls, overrides, and objection handling.</p>
<p>None of this shrinks the team. It gets the team measured, paid, and trained for the job it does now.</p>

<h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>Pull up last month's rep scorecard. Look at the first two rows: calls logged, quotes sent. Now ask how many of those quotes the rep actually built, versus how many your AI catalog assistant answered before the rep ever saw the thread.</p>
<p>For a growing number of distributors, the honest answer is most of them. The assistant takes a spec question at 7am, confirms availability, quotes a price off the buyer's own tier, and hands a ready account to a rep who hasn't picked up the phone yet. That used to be the rep's job. It still gets done, just not by a person.</p>
<p>The scorecard doesn't know that. Neither does the comp plan, or the 90-day ramp curriculum that still opens with two weeks of price-sheet memorization. All three were built around a version of the job that no longer exists on your floor. <a href="/will-ai-replace-b2b-sales-reps/">Whether AI replaces sales reps</a> is a question already settled for most teams: it doesn't, the work shifts. This post is the part that question skips. Skip the reset and the gap shows up first in your best rep's paycheck, then in your next hire's first ninety days.</p>
<p>ChatSKU answers first-touch. It doesn't run your sales team, and it was never built to. What follows is what changes on your side of that line.</p>

<h2>What should you measure once an AI catalog assistant handles first-touch?</h2>
<p>Retire the activity metrics that counted rep-performed first-touch work, and replace them with metrics that count what happens after the handoff.</p>
<p>Calls logged and quotes sent used to tell you something real. They measured work a rep did by hand. Once an assistant answers spec, price, and availability questions around the clock, those same numbers measure the assistant's work wearing a rep's name tag. A rep who looks busier on paper might just be sitting next to a tool that's doing more of the lifting.</p>
<p>Three numbers tell you what's actually happening instead:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Deflection rate.</strong> The share of first-touch volume your assistant resolves without a rep touching it at all.</li>
  <li><strong>Qualified-handoff rate.</strong> The share of assistant conversations that reach a rep already spec'd, priced, and ready to negotiate, not a cold "someone wants info" ping.</li>
  <li><strong>Time-to-quota.</strong> How fast a rep hits quota when their pipeline starts from qualified handoffs instead of cold inquiries.</li>
</ul>
<p>Track those three and you can separate a rep who's gotten better at closing from a rep whose pipeline just got easier. Blur that line and you reward the wrong behavior, or coach the wrong rep.</p>
<p>Here's where the data comes from. ChatSKU already distinguishes a buyer who's browsing from one who's ready to buy, and surfaces that intent signal through <a href="/features/">your catalog integration</a> into the dashboards you already run. That's a data input, not a finished report. Nobody ships a box labeled "deflection rate" out of the factory. You build the scorecard around the signal the assistant gives you, and that work is the manager's, not the software's.</p>
<p>An <a href="/ai-sales-assistant-b2b-ecommerce/">AI sales assistant built for first-touch</a> can only make this measurable if someone on your side decides to measure it differently. Most teams don't. They keep the old scorecard running next to a tool that broke its assumptions months ago.</p>

<h2>What should your comp plan pay for now?</h2>
<p>Pay for the close, the exception, and the save. Not for the first quote, because the first quote isn't a rep's work anymore.</p>
<p>The risk with comp plans isn't that you'll pay reps less. It's that you'll keep paying them for the wrong trigger. If commission still fires on "quote sent" as a rep activity, you've built an incentive for a rep to quietly redo work the assistant already finished, just to get credit for it. That's not laziness. That's a comp plan doing exactly what it was built to do, pointed at a job that no longer needs doing twice.</p>
<p>Reset the trigger to something a rep still controls. Closing the deal. Negotiating an exception. Saving an account that was about to walk. Those are still entirely human calls, and they're the calls that should still move a paycheck.</p>
<p>Credit allocation needs the same honesty. If the assistant surfaces a qualified handoff and a rep closes it, that rep earns real credit, not zero credit because a tool touched the lead first. A defined handoff-to-close credit splits that fairly. Skip this step and reps quietly work around a tool that punishes them for using it.</p>
<p>New hires need one more adjustment. Add a ramp kicker tied to closing qualified handoffs in the first 90 days. A new rep is the person most likely to miss quota while they're still building judgment, not catalog knowledge, and a kicker built around handoffs rewards the skill they're actually there to learn.</p>
<p>None of this fixes what happens after the quote goes out. <a href="/b2b-quote-to-order-automation/">Quote-to-order follow-through</a> already owns that gap, and resetting the comp trigger doesn't replace it. It just stops paying reps to recreate the problem by re-quoting work that's already done.</p>

<h2>What should a new hire's first 90 days look like now?</h2>
<p>Cut the weeks you used to spend on SKU and price-sheet memorization, and spend that time on judgment instead, because the assistant already holds the catalog.</p>
<p>The biggest single chunk of legacy ramp time went to memorizing specs, SKUs, and price sheets. That made sense when a new rep was the only source of that information for a buyer. It makes a lot less sense once an assistant holds and surfaces the same information, correctly, at 2am, without a training period.</p>
<p>That time doesn't disappear. It moves to the skill the assistant can't do: knowing when to override or correct an answer, handling an objection the moment it comes up, and reading how urgent an account really is during a live handoff. Those are judgment calls, and they only get built by practicing them, not by reading a spec sheet twice.</p>
<p>A concrete shift looks like this. Weeks one and two, a new hire shadows qualified handoffs instead of cold calls, watching how an experienced rep turns an already-spec'd lead into a close. Weeks three and four, they practice override and correction scenarios with a manager, since spotting when the assistant's answer needs a human adjustment is a skill, not an instinct. Week five on, they start closing supervised deals of their own, reviewed on the handoffs they're working, not the SKUs they've memorized.</p>
<p>New hires carry a real worry about where they fit once an assistant answers the easy questions. <a href="/what-is-agentic-commerce/">What agentic commerce actually means</a> for a catalog is worth pointing a nervous new hire toward directly, because the honest answer is that the assistant narrows the job. It doesn't remove the person doing it.</p>
<p>This shift also changes how fast you can grow the team. A <a href="/headcount-ceiling/">headcount ceiling</a> built around hiring ahead of order volume doesn't apply the same way once first-touch scales on its own. You can bring on one new hire and ramp them into judgment work right away, instead of hiring three reps to cover a volume spike that was mostly spec questions anyway.</p>

<h2>Why isn't there a clean benchmark for any of this yet?</h2>
<p>Because nobody has published a methodology-disclosed study tying AI-assisted first-touch to ramp time or quota speed for industrial B2B distributors specifically, and importing a number from a different industry will mislead you more than it helps.</p>
<p>Search for a ramp-time or quota-speed benchmark and you'll mostly find figures from software, pharma, healthcare, and food-tech companies, not industrial distributors with a five-figure SKU count and a decades-old ERP export. Borrowing their percentage tells you about their industry, not yours.</p>
<p>Even the more careful research can't answer the comp and KPI questions this post is about. A 2025 industry survey of US sales reps and managers found that incentive redesign, not the tool itself, is the biggest lever in whether an AI rollout changes performance, with most of the value sitting in people and process. It published no specific number for ramp time or comp. That absence is itself the finding: nobody has written the distributor-specific version of this yet.</p>
<p>So don't wait for someone to publish it. Track your own before-and-after baseline for 90 days: deflection rate, qualified-handoff rate, and rep ramp time, measured on your floor, with your SKU count and your buyers. A <a href="/roi-calculator/">model built around your own numbers</a> beats a borrowed benchmark from an industry that doesn't look anything like yours. You'll have a real number in three months. Right now, nobody has one to hand you.</p>

<h2>People also ask</h2>
<h3>Does an AI catalog assistant replace the need for a sales manager to set quotas?</h3>
<p>No. It changes what the quota is built from. A quota based on qualified handoffs looks different from one based on cold inquiries, but someone still has to set it and adjust it as volume shifts.</p>
<h3>How long does it take to see a difference in ramp time?</h3>
<p>There's no universal number yet, and borrowing one from software or pharma ramp studies won't describe your floor. Track your own 90-day before-and-after instead: deflection rate, qualified-handoff rate, and how fast new hires hit quota.</p>
<h3>Should experienced reps and new hires have different KPIs?</h3>
<p>Yes. Time-to-quota matters more for a new hire still building judgment. Qualified-handoff close rate matters more for a tenured rep who should already be closing most of what reaches them.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>How do we introduce new KPIs without alienating tenured reps?</h3>
<p>Frame it as crediting different work, not taking credit away. Show reps the qualified-handoff data before you change what they're judged on, so they see what's landing in their pipeline first.</p>
<h3>Should we change comp plans retroactively or only for new deals going forward?</h3>
<p>Only going forward. Changing commission retroactively on deals already quoted under the old trigger breaks trust fast, and that trust is exactly what a careless AI rollout already puts at risk. Set a clear start date and apply the new trigger from there.</p>
