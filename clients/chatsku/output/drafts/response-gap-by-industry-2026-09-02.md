### SECTION: Executive summary

<p>The response gap is not one number. Hardware, electronics, and industrial supply all lose deals to slow replies, but they lose them to three different clocks.</p>

<p>In hardware the clock is breadth. In electronics it is availability. In industrial supply it is downtime. The same four-hour delay is survivable in one, expensive in the second, and account-ending in the third.</p>

<p>That matters because most distributors measure a single average response time across the whole business. An average hides the segment where the delay actually costs you money.</p>

### SECTION: Introduction

<p>Ask three distributors what a slow quote costs them and you will get three different answers. The fastener supplier talks about volume. The component distributor talks about allocation. The MRO supplier talks about a plant manager who stopped calling.</p>

<p>They are all describing <a href="/what-is-the-response-gap/">the response gap</a>, which is how long a buyer waits between asking you something and getting a real answer. They are not describing the same problem.</p>

<p>This post breaks the gap down by segment. What the buyer is actually waiting for, what the delay costs, and which number you should be watching.</p>

<p>One thing to be straight about first. There is no credible published benchmark that measures response times separately for these three industries. What follows is a structural argument built on how each business actually works, not a table of survey figures we cannot source.</p>

### SECTION: Why does the response gap look different in every industry?

<p>Because the buyer is not waiting for the same thing.</p>

<p>A response gap is only expensive when the delay changes the outcome. What changes the outcome differs by what you sell. In one segment the buyer simply moves on. In another your answer goes stale while it sits in a queue. In a third the buyer is losing money every hour they wait.</p>

<p>So the useful question is not "how fast do we reply." It is "what is the buyer's deadline, and are we inside it." Those are different questions and they produce different priorities.</p>

### SECTION: Hardware and fasteners: the breadth clock

<p>In hardware the response gap is a volume problem hiding inside a workload problem.</p>

<p>Catalogs here run to tens of thousands of near-identical line items. Bolts, anchors, fittings, and brackets that differ by a thread pitch, a coating, or a grade. Unit values are low and requests usually arrive as lists rather than single items.</p>

<p>That combination produces a quiet failure. Quoting a forty-line list by hand takes real time, and the order it produces may be worth a few hundred dollars. A rep with a full inbox will work the large opportunity first. That is a rational choice and it is also why the requests easiest to win are the ones that sit longest.</p>

<p>The cost never appears as a lost deal you can name. It appears as volume that quietly went somewhere else, because the buyer needed availability and price on a list and took the first supplier who confirmed both.</p>

<p>The number to watch here is not average response time. It is the share of multi-line requests that get a complete answer on the first reply. Partial answers restart the clock.</p>

### SECTION: Electronics components: the availability clock

<p>In electronics the delay does not just annoy the buyer. It can make your answer wrong.</p>

<p>Component buyers are asking availability questions, and availability moves. According to <a href="https://www.ecianow.org/2026/06/25/semiconductorleadtimes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ECIA's breakdown of semiconductor lead times</a>, complex wafer fabrication alone takes up to 12 weeks, assembly and test add another 4 to 8, and the full cycle runs roughly 26 weeks.</p>

<p>Distributors in that channel hold around 180 days of inventory. Your buyer cannot influence any of it. They are working inside a cycle measured in months and trying to land parts against a build date.</p>

<p>ECIA also notes what happens when supply tightens: customers double order. That behaviour ripples through the channel as a bullwhip, and it means a position you quoted on Monday may not be the position you can honour on Thursday.</p>

<p>So the response gap here has a second cost on top of the lost sale. A slow reply produces a stale reply. The buyer who waits three days for a stock position, then finds it gone at the PO stage, learns something about you that a fast "no, but here is the alternate" would never have taught them.</p>

<p>The number to watch is the age of your answer at the moment the buyer reads it, not the time it took to write.</p>

### SECTION: Industrial supply and MRO: the downtime clock

<p>In industrial supply the buyer is not shopping. Something is broken.</p>

<p>An emergency MRO request is a symptom of a stopped line, and the economics of a stopped line are brutal. Senseye and Siemens put the scale of it plainly in <a href="https://assets.new.siemens.com/siemens/assets/api/uuid:1b43afb5-2d07-47f7-9eb7-893fe7d0bc59/TCOD-2024_original.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The True Cost of Downtime 2024</a>. The world's 500 largest companies lose almost $1.4 trillion a year to unplanned downtime, equal to 11% of their revenues.</p>

<p>Per hour, the numbers vary enormously by sector. An hour in a large automotive plant costs $2.3 million, more than $600 a second. At the other end, an hour in fast moving consumer goods costs about $36,000. The average large plant still loses 27 hours a month.</p>

<p>Read those figures carefully. They describe what your customer loses while their line is stopped, not how long distributors take to reply. That is exactly the point. The buyer's cost of waiting is orders of magnitude larger than the value of the order they are placing with you.</p>

<p>Which is why the response gap in MRO does not cost you a quote. It costs you the account. A buyer who could not reach you during a stoppage will make sure they never depend on you during the next one.</p>

<p>The number to watch is time to a usable answer during the buyer's emergency, not your average across all inquiries.</p>

### SECTION: Are you measuring the wrong clock?

<p>Most distributors track one average response time for the whole business. That single number is close to useless once you have more than one type of buyer.</p>

<p>An average blends a same-day MRO emergency with a routine fastener list and a component availability check. Improve the easy segment and the average moves. The segment that was actually costing you money stays exactly where it was.</p>

<p>Three questions worth asking at your next pipeline review:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Which segment has the widest spread?</strong> Not the worst average, the widest range between your fastest and slowest reply. Spread is where the losses hide.</li>
<li><strong>What percentage of first replies are complete answers?</strong> A holding message that promises a quote tomorrow has not closed the gap, it has documented it.</li>
<li><strong>What happens to inquiries that arrive after hours?</strong> A machine that fails at 6pm does not wait for your morning. Neither does the buyer.</li>
</ul>

### SECTION: What actually closes the gap in each segment

<p>The fix is the same shape in all three, but the priority order changes.</p>

<p>In hardware, the win is completeness at speed on long lists. That is a data problem before it is a staffing problem. If your catalog can be queried directly, a forty-line list stops being a forty-line task.</p>

<p>In electronics, the win is freshness. An answer that reflects the current position beats a more detailed answer written yesterday, and offering a named alternate immediately beats a perfect reply that arrives after the buyer has committed elsewhere.</p>

<p>In MRO, the win is availability at the moment of the emergency, which usually means outside business hours. That is the clearest case for something that answers when nobody is at a desk.</p>

### SECTION: People also ask

**H3: Which industry has the worst response gap?**

<p>There is no published benchmark that measures response times separately by industry, so any ranking you see is an estimate rather than a measurement. The more useful comparison is cost of delay. In MRO the buyer's hourly losses dwarf the order value, which makes the same delay far more damaging than it would be on a routine fastener list.</p>

**H3: Is the response gap just a staffing problem?**

<p>Usually not. Adding headcount raises capacity during business hours but does nothing for inquiries that arrive at night or on weekends. It also does not make a forty-line list faster to quote. <a href="/what-is-the-response-gap/">The full definition of the response gap</a> treats it as a timing problem for exactly that reason.</p>

**H3: What counts as fast enough on a B2B quote?**

<p>It depends which clock the buyer is on. An emergency MRO request needs an answer in minutes to be useful. A routine component availability check needs an answer while the position is still accurate. A multi-line hardware list needs a complete answer before a competitor sends one.</p>

**H3: Should we set different response targets for different product lines?**

<p>Yes, and most distributors do not. A single service level across a mixed catalog will be too slow for emergencies and unnecessarily strict for routine orders. Segment the target by what the buyer is waiting for.</p>

### SECTION: Conclusion

<p>Hardware, electronics, and industrial supply do not share a response gap. They share a symptom.</p>

<p>Work out which clock your buyers are on, then measure against that instead of a single company-wide average. The segment with the widest spread between your fastest and slowest reply is almost always where the money is going.</p>

### SECTION: Frequently asked questions

**H3: What is the response gap?**

<p>It is how long a buyer waits between raising an inquiry and receiving a real answer. Not an acknowledgement, an answer. We define it in full in <a href="/what-is-the-response-gap/">what is the response gap</a>, and the <a href="/response-gap/">response gap page</a> shows what closing it looks like in practice.</p>

**H3: Why can't we just measure average response time?**

<p>An average across a mixed catalog blends emergencies with routine orders. Improving the easy segment moves the average while the expensive segment stays broken. Track the spread and segment the measurement.</p>

**H3: Does this apply to distributors with only a few thousand SKUs?**

<p>Yes. How deep your variants run matters far more than how many items you list. A few thousand parts separated by grade, thread, or tolerance create the same quoting workload as a much larger catalog of plainly distinct products.</p>

**H3: How does after-hours inquiry volume change the picture?**

<p>It changes it most in MRO, where failures do not follow business hours, and least in routine hardware ordering. If you have never measured what share of your inquiries arrive outside working hours, that number usually surprises people.</p>

**H3: Do faster replies hurt quote quality?**

<p>They do if speed means guessing. They do not if the first answer comes straight from your catalog data rather than from someone's memory. The risk is not speed, it is answering from the wrong source.</p>

**H3: Where do RFQ workflows fit into this?**

<p>An RFQ form is where a qualified request gets captured, but the gap opens before that, while the buyer is still deciding whether you are worth asking. Our guide to <a href="/rfq-automation-manufacturers/">RFQ automation for manufacturers</a> covers the workflow side.</p>

**H3: What should we fix first?**

<p>Whichever segment has the widest spread between your fastest and slowest reply. That is where a fix produces the largest change, and it is usually not the segment your team complains about most.</p>
