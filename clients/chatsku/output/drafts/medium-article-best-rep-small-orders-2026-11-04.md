# Your best sales rep is spending half the day on $200 orders

*How quotes, price lookups, and catalog questions quietly eat the time of the people you hired to win big accounts.*

---

It's 10:40 on a Tuesday morning. Your best sales rep picks up the phone.

A buyer wants 500 zinc-plated bolts, 3/8 inch, net-30. Does she have them? What's the price? Can she send a quote?

Your rep opens the price list. Then the inventory screen. Then the customer's account notes, because this buyer has a negotiated rate. She checks lead time with the warehouse. She builds the quote in a spreadsheet and emails it.

Twenty minutes later, the call is done. The order is worth $214.

While she was on that call, a 40-line request for quote from a much larger account sat in her inbox, unopened. It will get her attention after lunch. By then, the buyer has asked two other suppliers.

Nobody did anything wrong here. That is what makes it expensive.

## Where does a rep's day actually go?

Ask your sales team how they spend a day. You will hear "selling." Watch for a week, and the picture changes.

Most of the day goes to answering the same few questions, one buyer at a time:

- Does this part fit my application?
- Is it in stock?
- How long is the lead time?
- What's my price?
- Can you send me a quote?

None of these needs a senior rep's judgment. All of them need access to the catalog, the stock numbers, and the right price list.

So a skilled person becomes a human search bar. They look things up, copy numbers into an email, and wait for the next call.

The cost isn't only the time. It's the work that doesn't happen: the follow-up on the big quote, the call to the account that went quiet, the conversation that would have grown an account.

## What does one manual quote really cost?

You can put a number on this in ten minutes.

**Step 1.** Time five quotes from start to finish. Include the lookups, the price check, the email, and the follow-up.

**Step 2.** Multiply the average minutes by the number of quotes your team handles in a week.

**Step 3.** Multiply by the fully loaded hourly cost of the person doing it.

Here is an example with made-up numbers, so you can see the shape. Replace them with yours.

> 12 reps × 8 quotes a week × 20 minutes = 32 hours a week of quote work. At $45 an hour, that is $1,440 a week, or about $75,000 a year, spent on answering questions the catalog already knows.

That is before you count the quotes that never get sent, because the rep was busy, and the buyer moved on.

## Why is the price never just one number?

Here is the part that makes B2B quoting harder than it looks from outside.

A single part can have several correct prices.

Your long-standing distributor has a rate you negotiated in March. A first-time buyer sees list price. A large account is owed a volume break that nobody remembers to apply. A contractor group might sit at one discount, a wholesale group at another, and a small set of strategic partners at a third.

Same part. Same catalog. Different answers, depending on who is asking.

A PDF cannot hold that. A website with one price cannot show it. So the price lives in the heads of your reps and in a few spreadsheets they trust.

Every time a buyer asks "what's my price?", someone has to work out who the buyer is, what group they belong to, and what applies. That is the 20-minute call.

And a guessed price fails both ways. Quote too high, and the buyer leaves. Quote too low, and your margin does.

## Why doesn't hiring more people fix it?

Adding reps adds capacity. It does not change the shape of the work.

New hires take months to learn the catalog and the pricing rules. Their answers vary. Two reps quote the same buyer differently, and the buyer notices.

And the questions keep coming at hours your team can't cover. The buyer who needs a part at 9 pm doesn't wait for your 8 am open. They ask the supplier whose website answers.

Hiring for repetitive questions is paying a premium for something a system can do.
